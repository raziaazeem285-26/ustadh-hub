Surah Al-Hujurat: The Surah of Manners

R Razia Azeem · 18 Nov, 2026

Eighteen verses, one code of manners — how to speak, how to verify news, how to protect a brother's honour, and what truly makes a person noble in Allah's sight.

In this article
  1. A Surah Named After Rooms
  2. Lower Your Voice — Manners Before the Prophet ﷺ
  3. Verify Before You Share — The News Rule
  4. Brothers First — Make Peace Between Them
  5. The Tongue and the Heart — No Mockery, No Suspicion, No Spying
  6. What Makes a Person Noble
  7. Seven Manners for This Week

Quran

Some surahs give us beliefs to hold. Others give us manners to live by. Surah Al-Hujurat does the second — in only eighteen verses, it builds a complete code of how believers should treat one another: how to speak, how to handle news, how to settle disputes, and how to protect a brother's honour. Scholars call it the surah of manners, and once you read it as one connected teaching, you will see why: every verse is a rule for the tongue, the heart, or the home.

This surah is short enough to read in a few minutes, yet it touches almost every tension of daily life — arguments that get too loud, forwarded messages that turn out to be false, two friends who stop speaking, a joke that goes too far, and the quiet pride that ranks people by tribe, wealth, or skin. It was revealed in Madinah, when the Muslim community was growing fast and strangers were becoming neighbours — and its guidance is what our group chats and gatherings need today.

1.A Surah Named After Rooms

Al-Hujurat means "the rooms" — the private apartments of the Prophet's household, peace be upon him, in Madinah. The surah is the 49th of the Quran, revealed in Madinah, eighteen verses in the 26th juz. Its name comes from verse 4: those who called out to the Prophet from outside his rooms instead of waiting patiently.

The name is a clue to the surah's method: it begins with manners toward the Prophet himself, then widens the circle to the community, then to all of humanity. Verses 1 to 5 teach respect for the Messenger; verse 6, how to handle news; verses 9 and 10, reconciliation and brotherhood; verses 11 and 12, guarding dignity from mockery, suspicion, spying, and backbiting; verse 13 opens the circle to all mankind — peoples and tribes made to know one another, the most honoured the most righteous. Verses 14 to 18 close by drawing the line between claiming faith and living it: manners are not decoration on faith; they are its proof.

2.Lower Your Voice — Manners Before the Prophet ﷺ

The surah opens with a command that still shapes how a believer carries himself: "Do not put yourselves forward before Allah and His Messenger" (Quran 49:1). Do not rush ahead with your own opinion where revelation has spoken. Do not decide first and ask later. Then comes the verse that gave the surah its weight in the hearts of the Companions: "Do not raise your voices above the voice of the Prophet, nor speak loudly to him as you speak loudly to one another, lest your deeds become worthless while you perceive not" (Quran 49:2).

The books of hadith preserve why these verses came down. A delegation from the tribe of Banu Tamim arrived in Madinah, and Abu Bakr and Umar — the two closest Companions — disagreed over whom to appoint as the tribe's leader. One suggested Al-Qa'qa' ibn Ma'bad, the other Al-Aqra' ibn Habis. Their discussion grew loud in the Prophet's presence, and the verse was revealed (Sahih al-Bukhari 4845, 4847). It was not even an argument about religion — only an appointment — yet the Quran treated raised voices before the Messenger as a matter of faith itself.

The effect on the Companions was immediate. Thabit ibn Qays, a man with a naturally loud voice, heard the verse and feared his good deeds had been wiped out. He stayed home, certain he was among the people of the Fire, until the Prophet sent for him and reassured him: you are not of the people of the Fire, but of the people of Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari 4846). After the verse, Abu Bakr spoke to the Prophet only in a whisper, the commentators report, and Umar lowered his voice so much that the Prophet had to ask him to repeat himself.

The Prophet ﷺ is no longer among us, but the manners remain. Do not rush ahead of the Quran and the Sunnah with a loud opinion. Do not raise your voice in the mosque, in a gathering of knowledge, or in an argument where respect has already left the room. Volume never won an argument; it only announced that the argument was lost.

3.Verify Before You Share — The News Rule

Then the surah turns to something we do a hundred times a day: "O you who believe, if an untrustworthy person brings you news, verify it, lest you harm a people out of ignorance and become regretful for what you have done" (Quran 49:6).

The commentators report that this verse was revealed when a man brought the Prophet a false report about a tribe, and an expedition was nearly sent on the strength of it. The rule itself is complete: before you believe a report, share it, or act on it, check it. The verse names the danger precisely — harm done out of ignorance, followed by regret. Reputations do not un-break. A screenshot sent in anger cannot be unsent.

Our age has made this verse more urgent, not less. A forwarded message arrives with no source, a voice note claims "someone reliable said", a clip is cut to make a person look guilty — and within minutes, hundreds have believed, shared, and judged. The rule is simple: who brought this news? Is the source trustworthy? Is there evidence? What harm will I do if it is wrong? If you cannot answer, do not share. Silence costs nothing; a false share can cost a person's name.

4.Brothers First — Make Peace Between Them

When two groups of believers fall out, the surah does not tell the rest of the community to pick a side. It tells them to make peace — and to make it justly: "If two groups of believers fight, make peace between them... then make peace between them with justice and be fair. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly" (Quran 49:9). Then comes the sentence that holds the whole surah together:

إِنَّمَا ٱلۡمُؤۡمِنُونَ إِخۡوَةࣱ فَأَصۡلِحُوا۟ بَیۡنَ أَخَوَیۡكُمۡۚ وَٱتَّقُوا۟ ٱللَّهَ لَعَلَّكُمۡ تُرۡحَمُونَ

Quran 49:10

"The believers are but brothers, so make peace between your brothers. And fear Allah, that you may receive mercy." Brotherhood in faith comes before tribe, nation, family name, and friendship group — so the verse commands reconciliation as a duty, not a favour. The one who walks between two quarrelling people and brings them back together is not interfering; he is obeying the Quran.

Peacemaking has its own manners: do not carry one side's words to the other, do not enjoy the story, hear both, speak fairly, and let the settlement be just even when justice favours the side you like less. The reward is named in the verse itself: mercy. Read more about the duty of brotherhood in Love for Your Brother: The Hadith That Measures Your Faith.

5.The Tongue and the Heart — No Mockery, No Suspicion, No Spying

Verses 11 and 12 are the surah's guard over human dignity. "Let not a people ridicule another people; perhaps they may be better than them; nor let women ridicule other women; perhaps they may be better than them. And do not insult one another, nor call each other by offensive nicknames" (Quran 49:11). Mockery is forbidden with a reason that should humble every joker: the person you laugh at may be better than you in Allah's sight. The nicknames verse covers the labels people hate — names that can follow a person for years after one careless joke.

Then the surah moves from the tongue to the heart: "Avoid much suspicion, for some suspicion is sin. And do not spy, and do not backbite one another. Would any of you like to eat the flesh of his dead brother? You would hate it" (Quran 49:12). Suspicion, spying, and backbiting are one chain: a bad thought leads to digging for faults, and digging leads to talking behind a back. The Quran's image for backbiting — eating the flesh of a dead brother — is the strongest image in the whole surah, and it is meant to disgust us out of the habit.

"Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him say what is good or remain silent." — Sahih al-Bukhari 6018

The Prophet ﷺ gave the same teaching in a single sentence: "Beware of suspicion, for suspicion is the falsest of speech. Do not spy on one another, and do not hate one another — but be brothers" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5143). And he made the standard unmistakable: "A Muslim is the one from whose tongue and hand the Muslims are safe" (Sahih al-Bukhari 10). Safety from your tongue is not a small virtue; it is the definition of a Muslim in this hadith. We have written in full about backbiting itself in Backbiting (Gheebah): The Sin That Eats Your Good Deeds — here the point is the system: mockery, suspicion, spying, and backbiting are one disease, cured by guarding the heart before guarding the tongue.

6.What Makes a Person Noble

The surah's most famous verse draws the largest circle of all:

یَـٰۤأَیُّهَا ٱلنَّاسُ إِنَّا خَلَقۡنَـٰكُم مِّن ذَكَرࣲ وَأُنثَىٰ وَجَعَلۡنَـٰكُمۡ شُعُوبࣰا وَقَبَاۤىِٕلَ لِتَعَارَفُوۤا۟ۚ إِنَّ أَكۡرَمَكُمۡ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتۡقَىٰكُمۡۚ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ عَلِیمٌ خَبِیرࣱ

Quran 49:13

"O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you. Indeed, Allah is Knowing and Aware." One origin — a single man and a single woman. Peoples and tribes exist so that we may know one another, not rank one another. Nobility is measured by one thing only: taqwa, consciousness of Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ said the same in his Farewell Sermon: no Arab is superior to a non-Arab, and no white person to a black person, except by taqwa — a saying preserved in Musnad Ahmad. We covered that sermon in full in Farewell Sermon: The Prophet's ﷺ Last Message. The surah applies the same rule inside the community: no mockery of an accent, a village, a job, a skin colour, or a family name. Rank people by anything other than character and consciousness of Allah, and you have left this surah's teaching.

7.Seven Manners for This Week

A surah like this is meant to be practised, not admired. Here are seven of its manners, one for each day of the week.

Pause before you forward. Check the source, the evidence, and the harm if it is wrong — then decide (Quran 49:6). Lower your voice in one disagreement. Say your point once, calmly (Quran 49:2). Make peace between two people. One message, one visit, one fair word (Quran 49:9-10). Drop one nickname. If someone dislikes it, stop today (Quran 49:11). Assume the best once a day. When a bad thought about a believer comes, replace it with a good excuse until you have proof (Quran 49:12). Defend an absent person once. When someone is backbitten in front of you, change the subject or speak up (Quran 49:12). Greet someone outside your circle. A person from another tribe, town, or background — because we were made peoples and tribes so that we may know one another (Quran 49:13).

The surah ends where it began: with the difference between claiming faith and living it. "The Bedouins say, 'We have believed.' Say, 'You have not yet believed; but say instead, we have submitted'" (Quran 49:14). Faith is not a word we say about ourselves; it is a life other people can feel — in how we speak, verify, reconcile, and protect one another's honour. Eighteen verses. That is all it took to teach a community how to live together — and all it takes to teach us.
