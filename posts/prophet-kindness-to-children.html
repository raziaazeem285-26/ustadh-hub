The Prophet’s Kindness to Children: Mercy That Shaped a Generation

R Razia Azeem · 7 Nov, 2026

If you want to know a person’s character, watch how he treats those who cannot repay him. Children cannot repay anyone — they have no money, no influence, no power to praise or punish. And it is exactly with children that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ showed some of his most beautiful mercy: kissing his grandchildren in public, cutting his own prayer short for a crying child, and serving ten years beside a boy without once saying a harsh word to him.

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was sent as a mercy to the whole world — that is how Allah describes him in the Quran:

وَمَا أَرْسَلْنَاكَ إِلَّا رَحْمَةً لِّلْعَالَمِينَ

Quran 21:107 — “And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds.”

But mercy is a word that is easy to say and hard to prove. The companions proved it for us by recording the small moments — the moments nobody would have invented, because they are too ordinary, too tender, and too unlike the way powerful men usually behave. This article gathers those moments: the Prophet ﷺ with children. If you are a parent, a grandparent, a teacher, or simply someone who meets children every day, these are the moments to learn from — because Allah also tells us that in the Messenger there is an excellent pattern for us to follow (Quran 33:21, in meaning).

1.Mercy is the measure

Before the stories, the rule. The Prophet ﷺ tied mercy to children directly to the mercy we hope to receive from Allah. Abu Huraira (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ kissed his grandson al-Hasan ibn Ali while al-Aqra’ ibn Habis was sitting beside him. Al-Aqra’ said, “I have ten children and I have never kissed any of them.” The Prophet ﷺ looked at him and said:

“Whoever does not show mercy will not be shown mercy.”

(Sahih al-Bukhari 5997; also Sahih Muslim 2318.) The sentence is short, and it is a complete system of belief: the mercy you give is the mercy you get. Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) narrated a similar scene: a Bedouin came to the Prophet ﷺ and said, “Do you kiss children? We do not kiss them.” The Prophet ﷺ replied, “What can I do if Allah has removed mercy from your heart?” (Sahih al-Bukhari 5998; also Sahih Muslim 2317.)

Notice what the Prophet ﷺ does not do here. He does not argue, and he does not shame the man in front of others. He states the truth plainly and leaves it to work on the heart. Mercy, in his teaching, is not a soft feeling that comes and goes — it is a quality Allah places in the heart, and its absence is a loss to be feared. A heart that cannot be tender to a child has lost something precious, and the first step to getting it back is to notice the loss. For a wider reflection on mercy itself, read Mercy (Rahmah): The Heart That Reflects Allah's Mercy.

2.A kiss in front of everyone

The kiss that started the conversation above was not a private moment. The Prophet ﷺ kissed al-Hasan in front of a guest — al-Aqra’ ibn Habis, a tribal chief. In the Arabia of that day, a leader did not show open tenderness to a small child in front of other men; many considered it beneath their dignity. The Prophet ﷺ did it anyway, in full view, and then defended it with the words we just read.

That public kiss teaches two things at once. First, affection for children is not weakness — the same man who kissed his grandson in the gathering was the man who led armies and faced down the Quraysh. Tenderness and strength lived in the same heart. Second, a child’s dignity matters in public, not only at home: when a child is kissed and greeted warmly in front of others, the child learns he is valued, and the adults watching learn how a believer behaves. His grandchildren al-Hasan and al-Husayn (may Allah be pleased with them both) were dear to him in a way everyone in Madinah could see — he played with them and carried them. The lesson is simple: let your children see your affection, and let others see it too. The Prophet ﷺ, the most dignified of men, kissed his grandson in front of a chief — and called it mercy.

3.He shortened his prayer

Perhaps the most striking of all these moments happened in prayer — the most serious act of worship, led by the Prophet ﷺ himself, in the mosque, in front of the whole congregation. Abu Qatada (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: “When I stand for prayer, I intend to prolong it, but on hearing the cries of a child, I cut it short, as I dislike to trouble the child’s mother” (Sahih al-Bukhari 707). Anas ibn Malik (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated the same practice: the Prophet ﷺ would cut his prayer short whenever he heard a child crying, so as not to put the child’s mother to trial (Sahih al-Bukhari 709).

Think about what this means. The Prophet ﷺ was leading the prayer. He had intended a long recitation. And he changed his plan — in the middle of worship, in front of everyone — because a baby was crying somewhere in the back rows and a mother was anxious. He did not ask the mother to leave. He did not ask the child to be taken out. He shortened his own prayer instead. The worship of Allah and kindness to a mother were not in competition in his mind; kindness was part of the worship.

This is a lesson for every mosque and every home. A crying child in the prayer hall is not a disturbance to be removed — it is a test of the congregation’s mercy, and the Prophet ﷺ passed that test by making room for the child, not by removing the child. And in the home, the same rule applies: when a child needs you, the prayer you shorten out of mercy is not a lesser prayer. It is the prayer of a heart that understands what Allah loves.

4.A child on his shoulder in prayer

He did not only make room for children in prayer — he carried them into it. Abu Qatada narrated that the Prophet ﷺ came out to lead the prayer carrying Umamah, the daughter of his daughter Zaynab, on his shoulder. When he bowed, he put her down; when he stood up, he picked her up again (Sahih al-Bukhari 516). A small girl, on the shoulder of the Imam, in the middle of the prayer — and nobody in that congregation thought it strange, because that was simply who he was.

And on another occasion, when he was prostrating, one of his grandsons climbed onto his back — and he stayed in prostration, lengthening it, until the child climbed down by himself. The narration is in Sunan an-Nasa’i (1141, graded sahih by al-Albani). He did not push the child off. He did not rush. He waited, in the most humble position of the prayer, until the child was done.

These two scenes belong together, because they show the same thing from two sides: the Prophet ﷺ did not treat children as an interruption to his worship. He treated them as part of it. The child on his shoulder and the child on his back were not obstacles to his prayer — they were, in that moment, the prayer itself, lived out in mercy. A parent who prays with a toddler climbing on them is not failing at worship. They are following the best example there has ever been.

5.Ten years without an “uff”

Anas ibn Malik (may Allah be pleased with him) came to serve the Prophet ﷺ as a boy of about ten, and he stayed in his household for ten years. At the end of that decade, he said something extraordinary: “I served the Prophet ﷺ for ten years, and he never said to me ‘uff’ (a word of annoyance), nor did he ever say about something I had done, ‘Why did you do this?’ or about something I had not done, ‘Why did you not do this?’” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6038; also Sahih Muslim 2309).

Ten years. A boy, in a busy household, carrying messages, serving guests, making the mistakes every child makes — and not once did the Prophet ﷺ scold him, shame him, or even sigh at him in annoyance. This is not a small detail. It is the hardest test of character there is: living with a child, day after day, year after year, and never letting frustration turn into a harsh word. The Prophet ﷺ passed it for a full decade.

He also gave the rule that explains it: “He is not one of us who does not show mercy to our young ones and does not honour our elders” (Jami at-Tirmidhi 1919, graded hasan). Mercy to the young is not an optional extra in Islam — it is part of what it means to belong to this community. A teacher who humiliates a student, a parent who screams at a child for a spilled glass, a relative who mocks a shy boy — all of them have stepped outside the Prophet’s ﷺ way, even if they pray five times a day. The measure of a believer is not only in his worship. It is in his tongue with the young.

6.What mercy to children looks like today

So what does all of this ask of us, in our homes, today? Allah says: “O you who have believed, protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones” (Quran 66:6, in meaning). Protection here is not only about teaching them the rules — it is about building a home they feel safe in, where the deen is taught with the same gentleness the Prophet ﷺ showed. And Allah reminds us that our children are both a blessing and a test: “Wealth and children are [but] adornment of the worldly life” (Quran 18:46, in meaning), and “your wealth and your children are only a trial” (Quran 64:15, in meaning). The trial is not the children. The trial is how we treat them.

Here are five small practices, taken straight from the stories above, that any parent or teacher can start today. First, show affection openly — kiss your children, hug them, greet them warmly in front of others, the way the Prophet ﷺ kissed al-Hasan in front of a chief. Second, make room for them in worship — let them stand beside you in prayer, let them come to the mosque, and never make them feel that their presence is a problem. Third, never use humiliation as a teaching tool — ten years without an “uff” is the standard, and a child corrected gently remembers the lesson, while a child shamed only remembers the shame. Fourth, play with them — the Prophet ﷺ was the most playful of men with children, and play is how a child learns that you are safe. Fifth, teach them by example before words — a child who watches you forgive, share, and speak kindly will learn more from one week of watching than from one year of lectures. For a related reflection on loving the Prophet ﷺ through following him, read Loving the Prophet ﷺ: Love Shown by Following His Sunnah; and for children who have lost their parents, read Caring for the Orphan: Close to the Prophet ﷺ in Paradise.

Children are not small adults, and they are not a problem to be managed. They are a trust from Allah — and the way the Prophet ﷺ carried that trust is the way we are asked to carry ours. Start today: go to your child, hug them, and tell them you love them. The Prophet ﷺ did it in front of a chief, in the middle of prayer, and for ten years without a single harsh word.
