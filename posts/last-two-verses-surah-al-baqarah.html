Last Two Verses of Surah Al-Baqarah: Enough for the Night

R Razia Azeem · 2 Nov, 2026

Every night, before the lights go out, there are two verses a Muslim can recite in about two minutes — and the Prophet ﷺ promised that they would suffice the one who recites them. They are the last two verses of Surah Al-Baqarah, and they hold a whole creed, a whole comfort, and a whole dua in a few short lines.

Surah Al-Baqarah is the longest chapter of the Quran — 286 verses that cover law, worship, family, finance and faith. And yet it does not end with a command or a warning. It ends with belief, reassurance and a dua. The final two verses, 2:285-286, are known to millions simply as "Amana ar-Rasul", from their opening words: "The Messenger has believed..."

Many Muslims recite them every night without ever stopping to ask why these two verses, of all the Quran, carry such a special promise. This article looks at what they say, what the Prophet ﷺ said about them, and how to build them into your night.

1.The two verses that close the longest surah

The placement itself is meaningful. Surah Al-Baqarah opens with guidance for the God-conscious and closes with the believers' own declaration of faith. Verse 285 describes the Messenger ﷺ and the believers believing in Allah, His angels, His books and His messengers, making no distinction between any of His messengers. Verse 286 then turns to the human condition: what a soul can bear, what it earns, and what it asks for when it falls short.

Right before them, verse 284 reminds us that to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and that He will call us to account for what is in our hearts. Read together, the ending of the surah moves from accountability to mercy: yes, you will be held to account — and here is the dua that teaches you to ask for pardon before that day comes.

2."They will suffice him": the promise of the night

The most famous narration about these verses is reported by Abu Mas'ud al-Badri (RA) in both Sahih al-Bukhari (5009) and Sahih Muslim (807):

"Whoever recites the last two verses of Surah Al-Baqarah at night, they will suffice him."

What does "suffice him" mean? Classical scholars, including Imam al-Nawawi in his commentary on Sahih Muslim, recorded more than one explanation, and they are not in conflict. Some said the verses suffice as protection through the night — from harm, from Shaytan and from calamity. Others said they suffice in place of standing in night prayer for someone who cannot pray that night. Still others said they carry both meanings at once: protection and reward.

That is why the narration has become a nightly practice for so many Muslims. It is not magic, and it is not a substitute for the five daily prayers. It is a promised sufficiency: recite them with understanding at night, and Allah's protection and reward cover what you cannot cover yourself.

3.A gift from beneath the Throne

The two verses were not given in the ordinary way of revelation. In Sahih Muslim (806), Ibn Abbas (RA) reports that the Prophet ﷺ was given two lights: Surah Al-Fatiha and the closing verses of Surah Al-Baqarah. And in another narration in Sahih Muslim (173), the Prophet ﷺ received three gifts on the Night Journey (al-Mi'raj): the five daily prayers, the closing verses of Al-Baqarah, and the promise that whoever from his ummah dies without associating anything with Allah will be forgiven.

Another narration, reported in Sunan al-Nasa'i and Sahih Ibn Hibban, describes the last verses as given "from a treasure beneath the Throne" — a treasure no prophet before him had been given. A further narration reported in Sunan al-Tirmidhi and Musnad Ahmad says Allah wrote a book two thousand years before creating the heavens and the earth, and that the devil will not come near a house in which these two verses are recited for three nights. These narrations explain why scholars speak of the verses as a special honour for this ummah, not merely as two more verses.

4.What the verses actually say, line by line

Verse 285 begins: "The Messenger has believed in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and so have the believers. All of them have believed in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers, saying: We make no distinction between any of His messengers." Then comes the believers' answer to everything Allah has commanded: "We hear and we obey." It closes with a plea: "Your forgiveness, our Lord, and to You is the final return."

سَمِعْنَا وَأَطَعْنَا

Those three words — "we hear and we obey" — are the posture of the whole surah in miniature. The surah's first pages describe people who heard and argued; its last verse describes people who heard and obeyed.

Verse 286 then gives the comfort: "Allah does not burden a soul beyond that it can bear." Whatever you are carrying — grief, debt, illness, a difficult marriage, a child who worries you — this verse says the load was measured for you. It continues: each soul earns only what it strives for, and it is accountable only for what it earns. Then it teaches the dua for our weakness: do not take us to task if we forget or fall into error; do not lay on us a burden like the ones laid on those before us; pardon us, forgive us, have mercy on us. You are our Protector, so help us against the disbelieving people.

The closing requests are so beloved to Allah that, in a narration in Sahih Muslim, each time the Prophet ﷺ recited one of these requests, Allah answered: "I have granted it." Read the verse slowly and you realise you are not merely reciting — you are asking, and being answered, line by line.

5.Making them your nightly habit

The practice is simple. After Isha, or when you lie down to sleep, recite verses 285 and 286 of Surah Al-Baqarah. If you do not know them by heart yet, read them from a mushaf or your phone until you do — they are short, and most people memorise them within a week of nightly reading. Pair them with what the Prophet ﷺ recited at night: Ayat al-Kursi (2:255) and the last three surahs, Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas. The last two verses do not replace those; they complete a nightly shield the Sunnah describes.

Teach them to your children as the first long passage they learn after the short surahs, and recite them aloud in your home for a few nights running — the narration about the house protected for three nights gives families a beautiful reason to start together. And if you miss a night, do not give up the habit: recite them the next night. A habit that survives a missed day is stronger than one that collapses at the first gap.

One caution the scholars note: "suffice him" is a promise attached to recitation with faith, not a charm. The verses protect the one who believes them, lives by "we hear and we obey", and still prays, still strives, still repents. They are a door of mercy, not an excuse to stop walking through it.

6.Living the verses: "We hear and we obey"

The true test of the last two verses comes in the morning, not at night. "We hear and we obey" is easy to recite in bed and hard to live at work, in the market, in a family argument. But the verses themselves give you the way back when you fail: the dua for forgetfulness and error is built into the same passage. You were never expected to be flawless; you were expected to return.

And "Allah does not burden a soul beyond that it can bear" reframes every hardship. It does not say the burden is small — it says you are bigger than it, with Allah's help. The person who believes that verse does not ask "why me?" in the same way. He asks: what is the ease Allah promised alongside this hardship, and what is the pardon I should be asking for tonight?

So tonight, before you sleep, recite the last two verses of Surah Al-Baqarah. Two minutes, two verses — and, by the promise of the Prophet ﷺ, enough for the night.
