Surah Ar-Rahman: The Question Asked 31 Times

R Razia Azeem · 4 Nov, 2026

Surah Ar-Rahman is the surah that asks you a question thirty-one times — and will not let you leave it unanswered. Its seas, its scales, its gardens and its refrain are all one argument: look at what you have been given, and say so honestly.

  2. The refrain: a question asked thirty-one times
  3. The balance of the heavens and the scales of justice

Quran

Some surahs you read. Surah Ar-Rahman reads you. It is the 55th surah of the Quran, seventy-eight verses long, and most scholars count it among the Makkan surahs — revealed when the message was still being rejected in Makkah, and when the question at its heart was most urgent: confronted with all of this, what exactly are you denying?

The surah does not begin with a command, a story, or a warning. It begins with a name: Ar-Rahman, the Most Merciful. And then it lists His favours, one after another, like someone laying evidence on a table — the Quran itself, the creation of man, the gift of clear speech, the sun and moon running on their courses, the stars and trees in prostration, the earth spread out for living things. Before a single warning arrives, mercy has already spoken.

1. A surah that begins with mercy itself

The opening verses are worth slowing down for. "The Most Merciful taught the Quran, created man, and taught him clear expression" (Quran 55:1-4). Notice the order. The Quran comes before the creation of man in this telling — as if to say that guidance is not an afterthought to our existence, but the very first mercy that gives it meaning. And the gift singled out after our creation is not strength, wealth, or beauty. It is bayan — clear expression, the ability to speak, understand, and be understood.

That ordering quietly reframes the whole surah. Every favour that follows — the heavens, the earth, the seas, the gardens — is being shown to a creature who was given the words to respond. The question of the surah is not addressed to stones or stars. It is addressed to beings who can answer. You were given speech so that gratitude would be possible. Silence, in this surah, is a choice.

There is a second mercy hidden in the opening too. The name Ar-Rahman is the broadest of Allah's names of mercy — mercy that reaches every living thing, believer and disbeliever alike, in this world. The surah will go on to speak of punishment and Paradise, but it insists on starting here: before anything else, you are a recipient of mercy you did not earn.

2. The refrain: a question asked thirty-one times

Then comes the verse that makes this surah unlike any other:

فَبِأَيِّ آَلَاءِ رَبِّكُمَا تُكَذِّبَانِ

"Then which of your Lord's favours will you both deny?" (Quran 55:13). It appears thirty-one times, woven through the surah after almost every favour mentioned — and the "you both" is deliberate. The surah speaks to two audiences at once: human beings and jinn. Both were given revelation, both are accountable, and both are being asked.

A narration reported in Sunan al-Tirmidhi (3291) carries a beautiful detail: when the Prophet ﷺ recited this surah to his Companions and they listened in silence, he told them that the jinn had answered better than they had — each time the refrain came, they replied, in meaning, "We do not deny any of Your favours, our Lord, and to You belongs all praise." Whether we recite aloud or listen quietly, that is the answer the surah is training us to give.

Notice what the refrain does not ask. It does not ask whether you believe in Allah, or whether you can list His favours. It asks which one you will deny — because denial rarely announces itself as atheism. It shows up as ingratitude: the health we treat as a right, the family we treat as background, the food we eat without a thought, the Quran we own and never open. Each repetition is a mirror held up after each blessing, before we have time to look away.

"Then which of your Lord's favours will you both deny?" — asked thirty-one times, so that no blessing passes without an answer.

3. The balance of the heavens and the scales of justice

After the opening, the surah turns to the sky: "The sun and the moon run on their fixed courses. The stars and the trees prostrate. He raised the heaven and set up the balance, so that you may not transgress the balance" (Quran 55:5-9). The universe, in this picture, is not random. It runs on measure — mizan — and human beings are told to keep that same measure in their own dealings: "Establish weight with justice, and do not fall short in the balance" (Quran 55:9).

This is one of the surah's most practical turns. Cosmic balance and market balance are the same word. The God who keeps the sun on its course is the God who watches the shopkeeper's scales, the employer's wages, the parent's fairness between children, the judge's verdict. To cheat in a transaction is not a small business matter; in this surah's logic, it is a rebellion against the order of the universe itself.

And there is a gentler reading too. If Allah has kept the heavens balanced for you without your help, you can trust Him with the parts of your life that feel out of balance. The same hand that holds the stars holds your provision. The surah moves from astronomy to ethics to trust without changing subject — because, in the Quran's view, they were never separate subjects.

4. Two seas that meet but never mix

"He released the two seas, meeting side by side. Between them is a barrier, so neither of them transgresses" (Quran 55:19-20). Then, from them both, "come forth pearls and coral" (Quran 55:22). Generations of readers have paused over this image — two waters that touch yet keep their natures, each yielding treasure.

Whatever the precise natural phenomenon a reader has in mind, the surah's point is theological, not geographical: difference is not a defect in creation. Two things can stand side by side, remain distinct, and both produce good. That is a useful corrective in a world that treats every difference as a threat — between peoples, temperaments, or even the different seasons of one person's life. The barrier in the verse is not a wall of hostility; it is the order that lets each thing be itself.

And the pearls matter. The surah does not only show us the seas; it shows us what comes out of them. Favours are not abstract here. They are harvestable — food from the earth, pearls from the sea, ships that sail "like mountains" (Quran 55:24). Gratitude, in Surah Ar-Rahman, is always gratitude for something you can touch, eat, wear, or sail on. It refuses to stay theoretical.

5. Everything perishes — except His Face

Halfway through, the tone shifts. "Everyone upon the earth will perish. And there will remain the Face of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honour" (Quran 55:26-27). After all that beauty, this is the surah's sobering centre: everything you have just admired will end. The gardens, the seas, the stars — and you. What remains is Allah alone.

That is not meant to depress; it is meant to free. If everything perishes, then nothing in this world can be your ultimate security — not wealth, not health, not reputation, not even the people you love most. And if only Allah remains, then He is the only safe place to put your ultimate trust. The verse that sounds like a warning is also the surah's deepest comfort: the One who gave every favour will still be there when every favour has passed.

From there the surah looks forward. "Every day He is engaged in some matter" (Quran 55:29) — Allah is not distant from the world He made; He is actively sustaining it, answering, forgiving, providing, moment by moment. Then the gardens: "For whoever fears standing before his Lord are two gardens" (Quran 55:46), and below them, two more (Quran 55:62) — gardens with flowing springs, fruits in pairs, reclining on green cushions. The surah that began with mercy ends in Paradise, and its final verse returns to where it started: "Blessed is the name of your Lord, full of Majesty and Honour" (Quran 55:78).

A word of honesty belongs here. You may hear Surah Ar-Rahman called the "bride of the Quran". That description rests on a narration scholars grade as weak, so this article does not build any claim on it. The surah needs no borrowed title — its own refrain, its own structure, and its own ending are honour enough.

6. Answering the question in your own life

So how do you answer a question asked thirty-one times? Not with a single grand gesture, but with a habit. Here is one simple way to live with this surah for a week:

First, recite it slowly, once. Surah Ar-Rahman takes only a few minutes. Read it in Arabic if you can, and with a translation you trust. Each time the refrain comes, pause — and answer it, quietly, the way the jinn answered: I deny none of Your favours, my Lord; to You belongs all praise.

Second, keep a favours list for seven days. Each evening, write down three favours from that day — not grand ones, ordinary ones: a meal, a phone call, a moment of calm, a problem solved. The surah's method is repetition; gratitude, like the refrain, works by returning again and again until it changes how you see.

Third, check one balance. The surah ties cosmic balance to human fairness. Pick one scale in your life this week — a debt, a promise, a division of work at home, a price you charge — and set it straight. That is not separate from worship; in this surah, it is worship.

Fourth, say it to someone else. Tell a child, a friend, or a family member one favour you noticed today, and ask them for one of theirs. Gratitude spoken aloud is harder to forget than gratitude merely felt.

The surah ends where it began, with blessing on the name of your Lord. Between that beginning and that end, it has asked you thirty-one times. It only needs one honest answer, given daily: none, my Lord. I deny none of them.
