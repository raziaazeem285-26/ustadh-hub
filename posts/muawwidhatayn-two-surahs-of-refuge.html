Al-Mu'awwidhatayn: The Two Surahs of Refuge

R Razia Azeem · 10 Nov, 2026

The Quran ends with two short surahs that begin with the same words: "Say: I seek refuge..." — a shield the Prophet ﷺ carried into every night and every morning.

In this article
  1. The Last Two Surahs of the Quran
  2. Surah Al-Falaq: Refuge from the Evils Outside
  3. Surah An-Nas: Refuge from the Whisperer Within
  4. “Nothing Like Them” — What the Prophet ﷺ Said
  5. The Nightly Practice: Three Surahs, Three Times
  6. How to Live with the Two Refuges

Quran

The Quran does not end with a command, or a story, or a warning. It ends with a refuge. The last two surahs of the Book — Surah Al-Falaq (113) and Surah An-Nas (114) — both begin with the same two words: "Say: I seek refuge..." Together they are called Al-Mu'awwidhatayn, "the two surahs of refuge". The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) once told his companion Uqbah ibn Amir (may Allah be pleased with him): "Do you not see that there have been verses revealed to me tonight the like of which has not been seen before?" — and he named these two surahs (Sahih Muslim 814).

Surah Al-Falaq has five verses; Surah An-Nas has six. Eleven short verses, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) said about them: "No one ever sought refuge (with Allah) by means of anything like them" (Sunan an-Nasa'i 5430). This article walks through both surahs verse by verse, gathers what the Prophet (peace be upon him) said about them, and ends with the simple daily practice he himself never left.

1.The Last Two Surahs of the Quran

Both surahs begin with a command to the Prophet — and through him, to every believer: "Qul", say. Refuge is not a feeling you wait for; it is a sentence you say. You turn to Allah, name Him, and then name the evil you fear. That is the whole structure of both surahs: the One you run to, then what you run from.

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ الْفَلَقِ

Quran 113:1

"Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of Al-Falaq" — the Lord of the daybreak, the One who splits the darkness open with light. And then:

قُلْ أَعُوذُ بِرَبِّ النَّاسِ

Quran 114:1

"Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of mankind." The Quran closes by teaching you to ask for protection in the plainest possible words, and to ask it from the Lord of the very things you fear. One more thing about these two surahs is worth knowing plainly. The books of tafsir record that the companion Abdullah ibn Mas'ud (may Allah be pleased with him) did not write them in his own personal copy of the Quran — he had understood them as supplications of refuge. Ubayy ibn Ka'b (may Allah be pleased with him) explained that the Prophet (peace be upon him) recited them as part of the Quran, and the consensus of the Ummah is that they are two surahs of the Book — the last two, recited by every Muslim child who learns to read the Quran. Surah Al-Ikhlas stands just before them, and the three together — Ikhlas, Falaq, Nas — became the Prophet's nightly shield, as we will see.

2.Surah Al-Falaq: Refuge from the Evils Outside

Surah Al-Falaq names four evils, and each one is an evil that comes at you from outside. "Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of Al-Falaq, from the evil of what He has created, and from the evil of darkness when it settles, and from the evil of the blowers in knots, and from the evil of an envier when he envies" (Quran 113:1-5, in meaning).

First, "the evil of what He has created" — a general refuge, covering every harmful thing Allah created: harmful animals, harmful people, harmful accidents. Then the surah gets specific. "The evil of darkness when it settles" — the night, when dangers hide and when fear grows; the Lord of the daybreak is the One who ends every night. Then "the evil of the blowers in knots" — those who practise magic, blowing on knots to harm others. Some reports in the books of tafsir connect these two surahs with the incident when the Prophet (peace be upon him) himself was affected by magic — Labid ibn al-A'sam is named in Sahih al-Bukhari 5763 — and the surahs were among what Allah sent down for his healing. That connection is reported, not certain; what is certain is that the surah names magic plainly and teaches you to seek refuge from it. And finally, "the evil of an envier when he envies" — envy, which we explored in our article on hasad, the disease of the heart, is named here as a real harm to seek refuge from, not merely a bad feeling to manage. Notice the order: creation, darkness, magic, envy. From the general to the hidden to the human. The Lord of the daybreak covers them all — because the One who splits the dawn can split open any darkness around you.

3.Surah An-Nas: Refuge from the Whisperer Within

If Al-Falaq is about the evils outside you, An-Nas is about the one evil that gets inside you. "Say: I seek refuge with the Lord of mankind, the King of mankind, the God of mankind, from the evil of the whisperer who withdraws, who whispers into the hearts of mankind, from among the jinn and mankind" (Quran 114:1-6, in meaning).

Look at how the surah names Allah three times before it names the enemy once: Lord of mankind, King of mankind, God of mankind. Three attributes, rising in closeness — He sustains you, He rules you, He alone deserves your worship — and then the enemy is named in a single phrase: "the whisperer who withdraws". The word used is al-waswas al-khannas — the one who whispers and then slips away the moment you remember Allah. He does not overpower you; he suggests, and when you turn to Allah he retreats. That is why the surah ends with a warning that the whisperer is not only from the jinn: "from among the jinn and mankind". A whisper can come from a friend, a screen, a crowd — any voice that quietly suggests what Allah forbade. The cure the surah teaches is not to argue with the whisper, but to seek refuge from the One the whisperer fears. And the greatest protection against a whispering heart is a heart that remembers: dhikr, by which hearts find rest (Quran 13:28), is the daily habit that leaves the whisperer nowhere to hide.

4."Nothing Like Them" — What the Prophet ﷺ Said

No two surahs in the Quran carry quite this weight of recommendation from the Prophet (peace be upon him) himself. Gather the reports together and a picture forms.

To Uqbah ibn Amir he said: "Shall I not teach you the best two surahs that can be recited?" — and he taught him Al-Falaq and An-Nas (Sunan an-Nasa'i 5436, graded hasan). When Uqbah did not seem overjoyed with two short surahs, the Prophet (peace be upon him) led the Fajr prayer reciting them, then turned and asked him what he thought. The lesson was in the recitation: these are not small surahs; they are the best two for seeking refuge. Another report in the same collection adds: "The people cannot seek refuge with Allah by means of anything better than these two" (Sunan an-Nasa'i 5429). Abu Sa'id al-Khudri (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Prophet (peace be upon him) used to seek refuge with Allah from the jinn and from the evil eye — until the Mu'awwidhatayn were revealed, and then he recited them and nothing else (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2058, graded hasan gharib). Before them he used other words of refuge; after them, he needed nothing else. That is the measure of these two surahs: they replaced every other formula of protection the Prophet (peace be upon him) had used. Ayat al-Kursi is the greatest verse of the Quran, and the last two verses of Surah Al-Baqarah are enough for the night — but for seeking refuge itself, the Prophet (peace be upon him) said there is nothing like these two.

"No one ever sought refuge (with Allah) by means of anything like them." — Sunan an-Nasa'i 5430

5.The Nightly Practice: Three Surahs, Three Times

The Prophet's practice with these surahs was not occasional. It was nightly, and it was exact. Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) reported: "Whenever the Prophet (peace be upon him) went to bed every night, he used to cup his hands together and blow over it after reciting Surat Al-Ikhlas, Surat Al-Falaq and Surat An-Nas, and then rub his hands over whatever parts of his body he was able to rub, starting with his head, face and front of his body. He used to do that three times" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5017).

Three surahs, three times, every night — cupped hands, a gentle breath, and a wipe over the body from head to toe. It is a practice a child can learn in one evening and a practice the Prophet (peace be upon him) kept until his final illness: when he was too ill to do it himself, Aisha recited the Mu'awwidhatayn and rubbed his own hands over his body, hoping for their blessings (Sahih al-Bukhari 5016). The same three surahs guard the morning and the evening too. Abdullah ibn Khubayb (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Prophet (peace be upon him) said: "Say: He is Allah, (the) One, and Al-Mu'awwidhatayn, in the evening and in the morning, three times, and that will suffice you against everything" (Sunan an-Nasa'i 5428, graded sahih). Three times after Fajr, three times after Maghrib, three times before sleep. That is the whole practice — nine short recitations a day, and the Prophet's own word that they suffice against everything. Two honest notes belong here. First, no fixed count beyond these is established: you will hear claims that reciting them seven times, or a hundred times, brings a named reward — no authentic hadith gives such a count, so do not attach numbers the Prophet (peace be upon him) did not attach. Second, seeking refuge through the Quran never replaces medical treatment: the Prophet (peace be upon him) used medicine alongside his duas, and so should we. The surahs are a shield for the soul; the doctor is a means Allah provided for the body.

6.How to Live with the Two Refuges

So what does it look like, to live with Al-Mu'awwidhatayn? Start tonight. After Isha, before you sleep, cup your hands, recite Al-Ikhlas, Al-Falaq and An-Nas, blow gently into your hands, and wipe them over your head, your face, and as much of your body as you can reach — three times, exactly as Aisha described it. Teach it to your children the same night: a child who learns this before sleep has a shield that will follow them into every hostel, every journey, every lonely night of their life. If you want your children to learn to read these surahs themselves, with a teacher who knows them by heart, you can begin with a free trial class of one-to-one online Quran teaching.

Then make it your morning and evening habit: three times after Fajr, three times after Maghrib — the two surahs and Al-Ikhlas together. And when fear comes in the daytime — a bad dream, a frightening headline, a whisper you cannot name — do not reach first for your phone. Say it out loud: "Qul a'udhu bi Rabbil-falaq." Say it in Arabic, or in your own words: "I seek refuge with the Lord of the daybreak." The Quran ends with a refuge because your life will need one every single day. The darkness settles, the whisperer whispers, the envier envies — and the Lord of the daybreak is still there, splitting the night open with light. That is why the Book ends where it ends: not with a command you might fail, but with a refuge you can always take. The virtue of reciting the Quran — ten rewards for every letter — means even these eleven short verses, read three times a night, are a mountain of reward besides their protection. Tonight, read them slowly, understand them once, and then never leave them again.
