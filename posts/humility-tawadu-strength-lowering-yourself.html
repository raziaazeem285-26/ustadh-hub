Humility (Tawadu'): Strength in Lowering Yourself Before Allah

R Razia Azeem · 30 Oct, 2026

The world teaches us to stand tall, speak loudly and never let anyone look down on us. Islam teaches something that sounds like the opposite — and turns out to be stronger. Humility, tawadu', is not thinking little of yourself. It is knowing exactly who you are before Allah, and letting that knowledge keep you gentle with people, whatever your wealth, knowledge or position.

Picture two people walking into a room. One walks in as if the room belongs to him — chest out, voice raised, eyes scanning for who matters and who can be ignored. The other walks in quietly, greets the people nobody else notices, and sits wherever there is space. The world calls the first one confident and the second one weak. Islam reverses the judgement. The quiet one is practising tawadu', humility — and in Allah's measure, he is the strong one. This post continues the virtues series on this blog — taqwa, steadfastness, mercy — with the virtue that guards all the rest from turning into pride.

Why does humility need guarding? Because every good thing you gain can become a reason to look down on others: knowledge, wealth, piety, a good family, even the fact that you pray and others do not. Humility is the virtue that stops your blessings from becoming your boast.

1. What Humility Really Is

Humility in Islam is not self-hatred, and it is not letting people walk over you. A humble person does not think he is worthless — he knows he is a servant of Allah, honoured by that servitude, and he knows that every good he has is a gift, not an achievement he invented. The classical scholars described it simply: humility comes from knowing Allah's greatness and knowing your own faults. When both are in view, arrogance has nowhere to stand.

That is why humility looks different from low self-esteem. A person with low self-esteem hides because he thinks he is nothing. A humble person serves because he knows he is something — a servant of the Most Merciful — and that his worth does not depend on being above anyone else. He can admit a mistake without collapsing, accept correction without resenting it, and celebrate another person's success without feeling diminished. Nothing about that is weakness. It takes a very secure heart to say, “You are right, and I was wrong.”

2. Walking Humbly on the Earth

Allah describes His beloved servants with a walk before He describes their worship: “And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily [with humility], and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace” (Quran 25:63, in meaning). The rest of the passage goes on to describe their night prayer, their balanced spending and their repentance — but it starts with how they walk. How you move through the world, how you carry yourself among people, is itself an act of worship.

“Walk upon the earth easily” does not mean shuffling along in fake meekness. It means a walk without swagger: no strutting to impress, no shouldering past people as if they were obstacles, no walk that says “make way for someone important.” And the second half of the verse is the test of the first: when ignorant people address you harshly, you answer with words of peace. Anyone can look humble when everyone is polite. Humility shows when someone insults you and you still do not reach for a sharper insult in return.

3. Luqman's Advice to His Son

Some of the clearest teaching on humility in the Quran comes not as a command to adults but as a father's advice to his son. Luqman tells him: “And do not turn your cheek [in contempt] toward people and do not walk through the earth exultantly. Indeed, Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful” (Quran 31:18, in meaning). Then he adds the positive side: “And be moderate in your pace and lower your voice; indeed, the most disagreeable of sounds is the voice of donkeys” (Quran 31:19, in meaning).

Notice how practical the advice is. It is not abstract philosophy about the ego. It is about your face, your walk and your voice — the three things people meet before they ever hear your opinions. Do not turn your cheek away from people in contempt, as if they were beneath your notice. Do not walk as if the earth were a stage. Do not shout to dominate a room. A father who teaches his son these three things is teaching him that manners are not decoration on faith; they are faith, made visible. Parents reading this will recognise the connection with teaching children well: the Quran and its manners are taught together, never apart.

4. Lower Your Wing

Allah commanded the Prophet ﷺ himself: “And lower your wing [i.e., be humble and show kindness] to those who follow you of the believers” (Quran 26:215, in meaning). The image is a bird folding its wing down over its young — protection, closeness, gentleness, not distance and display. And the same gentleness is praised in the Prophet's own conduct: “So by mercy from Allah, [O Muhammad], you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude [in speech] and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you” (Quran 3:159, in meaning).

This is the answer to the person who fears humility will cost him respect. The Prophet ﷺ was the most honoured of people, and his gentleness did not empty his gatherings — it filled them. People stayed because he was approachable. Harshness drives people away; humility draws them near. A teacher, a parent, a manager or an elder who lowers his wing — who listens fully, who does not humiliate a person for a mistake, who greets the junior before the senior — will find that people trust him more, not less. Authority built on fear lasts until the feared person leaves the room. Authority built on humility lasts because people choose to stay.

5. Allah Raises the Humble

The Prophet ﷺ gave humility a promise that turns the world's logic upside down: “No one humbles himself for the sake of Allah but that Allah raises him [in status]” (Sahih Muslim 2588, in meaning). The same narration joins it to two other promises: charity does not decrease wealth, and forgiving another person only increases your honour. All three break the same false maths — the idea that giving something away leaves you with less. Give wealth, and Allah increases you. Give forgiveness, and Allah honours you. Lower yourself before Allah, and Allah raises you.

Another narration makes humility a command between people, not only before Allah: “Allah has revealed to me that you must be humble towards one another, so that no one oppresses another or boasts to another” (Sahih Muslim 2865, in meaning). Arrogance is not a private feeling; it is the root of oppression and boasting. The person who thinks he is above others will eventually treat them as below him. Humility, by contrast, is what makes justice and mercy possible — which is why this virtue belongs beside them in this series. And the stakes are high: the Prophet ﷺ warned that no one who has even an atom's weight of pride in his heart will enter Paradise (Sahih Muslim 91, in meaning). Pride is not a small flaw to polish later. It is the one disease that can lock the door from the inside.

6. The Warning: Pride Brings a Fall

The Quran gives pride a face in Qarun, a man of immense wealth who, when his people advised him not to exult, answered that his riches came from his own knowledge (Quran 28:76-78, in meaning). He then paraded his wealth before his people in full adornment. The ending is swift and total: Allah caused the earth to swallow him and his home, and none of his wealth or followers could help him (Quran 28:81, in meaning). The very next morning, the people who had envied him the day before were saying, in meaning, that had Allah not favoured them, they too might have been swallowed — and that the disbelievers do not succeed (Quran 28:82).

Qarun's story is not only about billionaires. His sentence — “I was only given it because of knowledge I have” — is a sentence ordinary people say in smaller ways every day: I earned this alone; I deserve this more than others; my degree, my salary, my piety, my family name — mine. The moment a blessing is claimed as self-made, humility has left the room, and the fall, whatever its shape, has begun. The cure is the opposite sentence, said honestly: this is from Allah, and I am its trustee, not its owner.

7. Five Daily Ways to Practise Humility

Humility, like every virtue, is trained in small acts before it is tested in large ones.

1. Sit wherever there is space. Do not insist on the seat of honour, the front row, or being greeted first. Choosing the ordinary place on purpose, without resentment, is a daily workout for the ego.

2. Admit one mistake a day, promptly. Say “I was wrong” before anyone forces it out of you — to your spouse, your child, your colleague. Each admission loosens pride's grip a little more.

3. Listen without preparing your reply. When someone speaks, especially someone younger or less important in the world's eyes, give them your full attention. Interrupting to display your own knowledge is pride wearing a helpful face.

4. Serve someone who cannot repay you. Help the person who has nothing to offer back — a stranger, a cleaner, a child. Service without an audience is humility in its purest form, because there is no status to gain from it.

5. Thank Allah for every blessing by name. When something goes well, say it plainly: this came from Allah. Naming the Giver, out loud and often, is what stops a blessing from becoming a boast.

Humility will not make you smaller. It will make you the kind of person others feel safe around, the kind of person Allah raises, and the kind of person whose walk on this earth leaves no damage behind. “The servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily” — walk like that today, and answer harshness with words of peace.
