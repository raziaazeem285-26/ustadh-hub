Visiting the Sick: A Visit Allah Counts as Visiting Him

R Razia Azeem · 3 Nov, 2026

A short visit to a sick person carries a reward the Prophet described in astonishing terms — seventy thousand angels praying for you, and Paradise's harvest until you return. What visiting the sick means in Islam, and how to do it well.

  3. Seventy Thousand Angels and Paradise's Harvest
  4. What to Say at the Bedside

Daily Life

Most of us have felt it: the hesitation before visiting someone who is ill. Will I say the wrong thing? Will my visit tire them? Is a message enough? So we delay, and the delay grows, until the moment has passed and the visit never happens. Islam answers that hesitation with unusual force. Visiting the sick is not presented as a kind extra for especially caring people. It is presented as a right one Muslim owes another, a duty the Prophet commanded, and a deed whose reward is described in terms that stop you in your tracks.

This article gathers what the Quran and Hadith teach about visiting the sick: why it ranks so highly, what to say at the bedside, how to visit well, and what to do when you cannot go in person. It is a small deed with an enormous return — and unlike many good deeds, it costs almost nothing except a little time and a caring heart.

1.A Right, Not a Favour

The Prophet (peace be upon him) listed visiting the sick among the five rights of one Muslim over another. In a hadith reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, Abu Hurairah (may Allah be pleased with him) narrates that the Prophet said, in meaning: every Muslim has five rights over another Muslim — to return the greeting of peace, to visit the sick, to follow the funeral procession, to accept an invitation, and to respond to the one who sneezes and praises Allah. Notice the company visiting the sick keeps. It sits alongside the greeting of peace and the funeral prayer — the basic courtesies and duties that hold a community together. It is not charity work for the especially pious; it is part of what it means to belong to one another.

Another narration makes the command even more direct. Al-Bara' ibn 'Azib (may Allah be pleased with him) reports that the Messenger of Allah commanded his companions to visit the sick, to follow funerals, to respond to the sneezer, to help the one who fulfils an oath, to help the oppressed, to accept invitations, and to spread greetings of peace (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). And Abu Musa al-Ash'ari (may Allah be pleased with him) reports the Prophet's threefold command, in meaning: visit the sick, feed the hungry, and arrange the release of the captive (Sahih al-Bukhari). Three deeds, one spirit: a believer does not walk past human need. Our article on the rights of the neighbour explores the same spirit in the house next door.

2.You Would Have Found Me With Him

The most moving narration on this subject is a hadith qudsi — words in which the Prophet reports what Allah Himself says. On the Day of Resurrection, Allah will say to a person, in meaning: O son of Adam, I was ill, but you did not visit Me. The person will reply in astonishment: My Lord, how could I visit You, when You are the Lord of the worlds? Allah will answer, in meaning: did you not know that such-and-such a servant of Mine was ill, and you did not visit him? Did you not know that if you had visited him, you would have found Me with him? (Sahih Muslim).

"O son of Adam, I was ill, but you did not visit Me... Did you not know that if you had visited him, you would have found Me with him?" — Hadith Qudsi, Sahih Muslim, in meaning

Read that slowly, because it changes the whole picture of a sick visit. The visit is not a social obligation to be discharged, nor a favour to be counted. It is a meeting with Allah's mercy itself. The sick person is not an interruption to your day; in this narration, Allah places Himself, in His mercy and reward, at the bedside. That is why the scholars say that visiting the sick is among the deeds that bring a person closest to Allah — not because the act is grand, but because Allah has honoured it with His presence in this narration.

The Quran points in the same direction when Ibrahim (peace be upon him) describes Allah, in meaning: and when I am ill, it is He who cures me (Quran 26:80). Healing belongs to Allah alone, and the visitor's role is humble: to remind the sick person of that truth, to pray for them, and to leave them a little stronger than they were found. The visitor does not heal; Allah heals. The visitor witnesses, comforts, and prays.

3.Seventy Thousand Angels and Paradise's Harvest

If the hadith qudsi gives the visit its meaning, other narrations give it its measure — and the measure is staggering. 'Ali ibn Abi Talib (may Allah be pleased with him) reports that the Prophet said, in meaning: when a Muslim visits a sick Muslim in the morning, seventy thousand angels pray for his forgiveness until the evening; and if he visits in the evening, seventy thousand angels pray for his forgiveness until the morning — and he will have a harvest of fruits in Paradise (Sunan al-Tirmidhi, graded hasan).

Thawban (may Allah be pleased with him) reports another image just as vivid: the Prophet said, in meaning, that a Muslim visiting his sick brother remains in the khurfah of Paradise — its harvest of fresh fruits — until he returns (Sahih Muslim). Picture that. From the moment you set out for the visit until the moment you come home, you are described as walking through Paradise's garden, gathering its fruit. A hadith reported in Musnad Ahmad and Muwatta Imam Malik adds a further detail, in meaning: when a person sets out to visit the sick, he enters into Allah's mercy, and when he sits beside the sick person, he is immersed in it completely.

These narrations do not describe a long pilgrimage or an expensive charity. They describe an ordinary visit: walking to someone's house, sitting beside them, saying a few kind words, and going home. The reward is out of all proportion to the effort — and that is precisely the point: Allah's generosity is measured by His mercy, not by our effort.

4.What to Say at the Bedside

Many people avoid visiting the sick simply because they do not know what to say. The Prophet's own practice gives us words that are short, warm, and free of the awkwardness we fear. When he visited a sick person, he would say, in meaning: no harm — it is a purification, if Allah wills (Sahih al-Bukhari). "La ba'sa, tahurun in sha Allah." That single sentence does three things at once: it reassures, it gives the illness meaning as a cleansing, and it leaves the outcome to Allah's will. It is the perfect thing to say, and it takes five seconds.

Another dua the Prophet taught for the sick is reported in Sunan Abi Dawud and Sunan al-Tirmidhi: whoever visits a sick person who is not at the point of death and says seven times, in meaning, "I ask Allah the Mighty, the Lord of the Mighty Throne, to heal you," Allah will heal him from that illness. The Arabic is: "As'alullah al-'Azim, Rabb al-'Arsh al-'Azim, an yashfiyak." Saying it seven times takes less than a minute, and it turns a visit from small talk into an act of worship.

Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) reports that when someone fell ill, the Prophet would pass his right hand over them and say, in meaning: O Lord of humanity, remove the suffering, bring the cure — there is no cure but Your cure, a cure that leaves no illness (Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim). You do not need to perform a ritual or recite at length. A short, sincere dua, said aloud so the sick person hears it, is worth more than an hour of anxious conversation. The sick person should leave your visit feeling prayed for, not merely chatted to.

5.The Manners of a Good Visit

Good intentions are not enough; a visit should also be a mercy in practice. The scholars and the Prophet's example give us a few simple manners. First, keep it short unless the sick person clearly wants you to stay. A sick body tires quickly, and a visitor who lingers out of politeness can become a burden. Second, do not interrogate about diagnosis or prognosis, and do not offer frightening stories about other people's illnesses. Third, bring ease, not demands: a small useful gift, a cooked meal for the family, help with an errand — these speak louder than advice.

Fourth, watch your words about the illness itself. Never suggest the sickness is a punishment, and never promise a cure. The Quran reminds us that trials come to all of us, in meaning: We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient (Quran 2:155). Patience is the sick person's own virtue to practise, and our article on tawakkul — trusting Allah after doing your part explains how trust and patience work together. Our job as visitors is to strengthen that patience, not to test it with careless remarks.

Fifth, remember that mercy is not limited to Muslims. The Prophet visited a sick Jewish boy, and the visit became the means of the boy accepting Islam (Sahih al-Bukhari, in the well-known narration). Visiting a sick neighbour, colleague, or classmate of another faith is part of the same sunnah of mercy — kindness shown to a human being in hardship. And sixth, when you leave, leave a prayer behind: pray aloud for the sick person before you go, so they hear it, and continue praying for them after you leave. A visit ends; a dua does not have to.

6.When You Cannot Visit in Person

Distance, illness of your own, hospital rules, or a patient's need for rest can all make a visit impossible — and Islam does not make the reward depend on what you cannot do. A phone call made with sincerity, a message that says "I am praying for you" and means it, a meal sent to the family, help with the children or the bills: all of these carry the spirit of the visit. That finding is not limited to a chair beside the bed; it is found in every sincere act of care.

There is also a quieter truth worth remembering. The sick person is not only receiving; in the eyes of faith, they are also being honoured. Sickness itself, borne with patience, wipes away sins — a narration in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim teaches, in meaning, that no fatigue, disease, sorrow, sadness, hurt, or distress befalls a Muslim, even the prick of a thorn, except that Allah expiates some of his sins because of it. When you visit, you are not doing a favour for someone beneath you. You are sitting beside someone whom Allah is purifying, and whose duas — the duas of the tested and the patient — carry a special weight. Ask them to pray for you before you leave. It is a beautiful reversal: the visitor came to give, and leaves having received.

So this week, think of one person who is unwell — a relative, a neighbour, an old friend, a member of your masjid community — and go. Or call, if you cannot go. Say the short dua, keep the visit light, and leave them feeling remembered. In a world where the sick are so easily forgotten behind closed doors, your visit is a small act of faith with a reward of seventy thousand angels.
