Musa and Khidr: The Journey to Knowledge You Cannot See

R Razia Azeem · 20 Nov, 2026

On Fridays Muslims open Surah Al-Kahf, and the surah's third story is a journey: Prophet Musa (AS) travelled to learn from a servant Allah had taught — a boat damaged, a boy taken, a wall rebuilt, and the patience to wait for the reason.

Seerah & Stories

On Fridays Muslims across the world open Surah Al-Kahf — four stories, four tests: faith, wealth, knowledge, and power. This morning's article told the first — the young believers who slept in a cave for 309 years. The afternoon told the second — the parable of the two gardens. This is the third: the journey of Prophet Musa (AS) with a servant whom Allah had taught knowledge from His own presence, told in Quran 18:60-82.

1.The Question That Started the Journey

The story begins with a question. Prophet Musa (AS) stood before the Children of Israel, and someone asked him: "Who is the most learned person among the people?" He answered: "I am." The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ told this part himself, in the long hadith of Ubayy ibn Ka'b (Sahih al-Bukhari 4725; Sahih Muslim 2380a): Allah admonished Musa for not referring the knowledge back to Him, and revealed that a servant at the junction of the two seas was more learned than he was.

Musa was a prophet who spoke with Allah directly — his life is told in our article on Prophet Musa — but knowledge belongs to Allah, and even the most learned human must say: Allah knows best. The moment Musa answered without that phrase, Allah taught him, and through him us, that there is always knowledge beyond our own.

Musa did not hesitate. "O my Lord, how can I meet him?" Allah gave him a sign: take a fish in a basket, and wherever you lose it, he will be there. And Musa made a vow:

وَإِذۡ قَالَ مُوسَىٰ لِفَتَىٰهُ لَاۤ أَبۡرَحُ حَتَّىٰۤ أَبۡلُغَ مَجۡمَعَ ٱلۡبَحۡرَیۡنِ أَوۡ أَمۡضِیَ حُقُبࣰا

Quran 18:60

"And [remember] when Musa said to his boy-servant: 'I will not give up [travelling] until I reach the junction of the two seas or [until] I spend years and years in travelling.'" He set out with his young servant — the hadith tells us he was Yusha' ibn Nun — carrying a salted fish in a basket. One thing should be said plainly: the Quran never names the servant — he is called al-Khidr in the hadith (Bukhari 3402: he sat on dry, white land and it turned green behind him). The scholars differ on whether he was a prophet or a righteous servant; the Quran calls him only "a servant from among Our servants" (18:65). The "junction of the two seas" is never identified by place.

2.The Sign of the Lost Fish

The two travellers walked until they reached a rock, where they lay down their heads and slept. While they slept, the fish slipped out of the basket and took its way into the sea — and the Quran says it took its path "as in a tunnel" (Quran 18:61).

When Musa woke, his servant forgot to tell him about the fish — "none made me forget it except Satan", the young man later says (Quran 18:63) — and they walked on for the rest of the day and the whole night. Only the next morning, when Musa asked for food and spoke of their fatigue, did the servant remember. The hadith notes Musa felt no fatigue at all until he had passed the place he had been ordered to seek — the tiredness itself was the sign.

"That is what we were seeking," Musa said at once (Quran 18:64). He did not argue, and he did not blame the young man. He turned around and retraced his footsteps to the rock.

3.The Meeting at the Junction of Two Seas

At the rock they found him, a man covered with a garment. After the greeting — "Is there such a greeting in your land?" — Musa said, "I am Musa of the Children of Israel, and I have come so that you may teach me." The Quran describes the meeting in one verse that holds the whole story:

فَوَجَدَا عَبۡدࣰا مِّنۡ عِبَادِنَاۤ ءَاتَیۡنَـٰهُ رَحۡمَةࣰ مِّنۡ عِندِنَا وَعَلَّمۡنَـٰهُ مِن لَّدُنَّا عِلۡمࣰا

Quran 18:65

"So they found a servant from among Our servants, to whom We had given mercy from Us and had taught him from Us a [certain] knowledge." Two gifts are named before any lesson: mercy, then knowledge. His knowledge was not the knowledge of books or law. It was knowledge of what Allah had decreed behind the veil.

But the teacher set a condition before a single lesson: "Indeed, you will never be able to have patience with me" (Quran 18:67) — "and how can you have patience for what you do not encompass in knowledge?" (18:68). Musa promised patience, if Allah wills (18:69), and the servant said: "Then if you follow me, do not ask me about anything until I myself speak to you concerning it." (18:70) Three things were agreed before they took a single step: patience, obedience, and silence until the explanation comes.

4.The Boat — When Kindness Looks Like Damage

They walked along the seashore until a boat passed, and the crew — recognising al-Khidr — took them aboard free of charge. No sooner were they aboard than al-Khidr pulled out one of the planks with an adze. Musa could not stay silent: "These people gave us a free lift, yet you have scuttled their boat so as to drown its people! Truly, you have done a dreadful thing." (Quran 18:71)

The boat belonged to poor people who had just done them a kindness. The servant reminded him of the agreement: "Did I not say that you will never be able to have patience with me?" (Quran 18:72) Musa apologised — "Call me not to account for what I forgot" (Quran 18:73) — and the Prophet ﷺ said his first excuse was forgetfulness.

Then came the image that has carried this story for fourteen centuries: a sparrow dipped its beak once into the sea, and al-Khidr said:

"My knowledge and your knowledge, compared to Allah's knowledge, is like what this sparrow has taken out of the sea." — Sahih al-Bukhari 4725

The whole sea, and one beakful of water — that is all human knowledge against what Allah knows. The explanation of the boat came only later: it belonged to poor people working in the sea, and a king after them was seizing every good ship by force — so the defect was made to save it (Quran 18:79). A damaged boat is a boat a king will not steal. The defect was protection, not cruelty — and the poor sailors never knew how the damage saved them. How many of our own losses were the plank pulled out of our boat so that something worse would pass us by?

5.The Boy — The Hardest Test of Patience

They left the boat, and al-Khidr saw a boy playing with other boys. He took the boy's head in his hands and killed him. Musa cried out: "Have you killed a pure soul who has killed nobody? Truly, you have done a dreadful thing." (Quran 18:74) The servant answered again, more firmly: "Did I not tell you that you will never be able to have patience with me?" (Quran 18:75) Musa said, "If I ask you about anything after this, then do not keep me as a companion. You have received an excuse from me." (Quran 18:76)

This is the hardest part of the story, and it must be faced honestly. The Quran gives the reason only at the end: his parents were believers, and it was feared he would overburden them by transgression and disbelief — so their Lord would substitute one better in purity and nearer to mercy (Quran 18:80-81). The hadith's report of Ibn Abbas adds that the boy was a disbeliever while his parents were believers (Sahih al-Bukhari 4725).

Three things must be said plainly. First, the Quran names neither the boy nor his parents, and gives no age or place. Second, al-Khidr acted on a command from Allah — "I did not do it of my own accord" (Quran 18:82) — and no human being may take this story as a licence to harm anyone. Third, the point for us is not the act, but Musa's reaction: the pain of watching something terrible happen without knowing why, and the patience to wait for the explanation. Every believer has stood where Musa stood. The story does not answer every why — it teaches us to hold the question until Allah gives the answer, in this life or the next.

6.The Wall — Kindness for People Who Refused You

They walked on to a town and asked for food. The people refused to host them — not a single meal. In that same town they found a wall about to fall, and al-Khidr set it straight with his own hands. Musa objected a third time: "If you had wished, you could have taken payment for it." (Quran 18:77) The town had given them nothing, yet the servant worked for free, for people who had refused them. And the servant said the words that ended the journey: "This is the parting between me and you." (Quran 18:78)

The explanation of the wall is the gentlest of the three: it belonged to two orphan boys, and beneath it lay a treasure for them, and their father was a righteous man — so their Lord intended that they reach maturity and extract their treasure, as a mercy from Him (Quran 18:82). The wall was not repaired for the town, but for two orphans who did not know it existed — because their father had been righteous, and a parent's righteousness reached his children years after his death, in a wall rebuilt by a stranger who asked for nothing. The mercy was for the orphans, and it arrived exactly on time.

وَمَا فَعَلۡتُهُۥ عَنۡ أَمۡرِیۚ ذَ ٰ⁠لِكَ تَأۡوِیلُ مَا لَمۡ تَسۡطِع عَّلَیۡهِ صَبۡرࣰا

Quran 18:82

"And I did not do it of my own accord." Every act — the boat, the boy, the wall — was done by Allah's command, not by the servant's choice. The journey ends where it began: what we see is never the whole story, and the One who sees the whole story is Allah alone.

7.What the Journey Teaches Us

The Prophet ﷺ ended the hadith with a wish that tells us how to read this story: "We wished that Musa had been more patient, so that Allah might have told us more of their story." The journey was cut short by the student's impatience — even a prophet's. That is the first lesson: patience is the condition of learning. Musa promised patience and broke the promise twice, and the third time the journey ended. How many lessons have we lost the same way — by demanding the explanation before the lesson is finished?

The second lesson is about knowledge itself. The sparrow's beakful is the measure of all human knowledge against Allah's. Musa knew the law; al-Khidr knew the hidden decree. Neither was wrong, and neither was complete. The believer's answer to any question he cannot answer is the one Musa forgot at the start: Allah knows best.

The third lesson is the one the story leaves in our hands. We will all watch boats being damaged — a job lost, a plan ruined — and stand in towns that refuse us food. The story does not promise the explanation in this life; for the sailors it never came. But it promises that the One who pulls the plank has a reason, and the reason is mercy: a king turned away, a treasure protected, a father's righteousness reaching his children. The practice is simple: when you cannot see the reason, hold your patience, keep walking, and say with Musa — "I will not give up travelling until I reach" the place where the meaning becomes clear.
