Honouring the Guest: A Forgotten Sunnah of Islamic Hospitality

R Razia Azeem · 2 Nov, 2026

A guest at your door is not an interruption — in Islam, honouring him is a mark of faith. What the Quran and the Prophet teach about hospitality, and how to bring this sunnah back home.

  2. Ibrahim's Guests: The Quran's Picture of Hospitality
  3. What Honouring a Guest Actually Means

Daily Life

There was a time when a guest arriving unannounced was a cause for joy, not anxiety. The best food in the house came out, the cleanest bedding was laid, and the host's face lit up before a single word was spoken. Today, a knock at the door more often brings a different reaction: a glance at the clock, a worry about the state of the house, a quiet calculation of the cost. Hospitality has not disappeared, but it has become something we schedule, budget and sometimes dread — and in that change, something precious has been lost. Islam treats hospitality very differently. It does not present honouring a guest as a nice social habit or a cultural extra. It presents it as a mark of faith itself.

That is the starting point of this article, and it changes everything that follows. If hospitality is a matter of faith, then it is not measured by the grandeur of the meal or the size of the house. It is measured by the warmth of the welcome, the sincerity of the effort, and the ease with which a guest is made to feel at home. A cup of tea offered with a smile can carry more reward than a feast offered with a sigh.

1.A Mark of Faith, Not Just Good Manners

The Prophet (peace be upon him) joined hospitality to belief in the most direct way possible. In a hadith reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, he said, in meaning: whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him honour his guest. The same narration joins two other marks of the believer: speaking good or remaining silent, and honouring one's neighbour. Notice what this grouping does. It places hospitality alongside speech and neighbourliness — the everyday tests of character that no one sees on a stage, but that Allah sees in full. A person's faith is not only proved in the mosque; it is proved at the front door.

Another narration in the same collections gives hospitality a measure. The guest's honoured provision — the best of what the host can offer — is for a day and a night, and hospitality itself is three days; what is beyond that is charity. And the narration adds a limit on the other side: it is not right for a guest to stay so long that he burdens his host. The balance is beautiful and practical. The host is told to be generous; the guest is told not to overstay; and both are told that generosity has a shape — it is warm, it is real, and it is not endless obligation. 2.Ibrahim's Guests: The Quran's Picture of Hospitality

Long before any hadith on the subject, the Quran gives us the perfect picture of a host. In Surah Adh-Dhariyat, Allah asks, in meaning: has the story reached you of the honoured guests of Ibrahim? (Quran 51:24). The guests arrived, greeted him with peace, and he returned their greeting. Then, without fuss and without asking what they wanted, he went quietly to his household and returned with a fat roasted calf (Quran 51:26-27). He offered it and said, in meaning, will you not eat? The guests, as the story unfolds, were angels — but Ibrahim did not know that. He honoured strangers exactly as he would have honoured anyone.

"Has the story reached you of the honoured guests of Ibrahim? When they entered upon him and said, 'Peace,' he said, 'Peace, a people unknown.' Then he went to his household and came with a fat roasted calf." — Quran 51:24-27, in meaning

Every detail of this short scene is a lesson. Ibrahim did not keep his guests waiting while he debated what to serve. He did not offer excuses about what the house lacked. He gave the best he had, quickly and quietly, without making a show of it. The Quran calls these guests "honoured" — mukramin — and the word attaches as much to the host's treatment of them as to who they were. A similar account appears in Surah Hud (Quran 11:69-70), where Ibrahim again hastens to bring a roasted calf for his visitors. Two surahs, one habit: the friend of Allah was, above all, a generous host.

There is a deeper point here. Ibrahim's guests brought him news — the glad tidings of a son — but the blessing arrived after the hospitality, not before it. He did not wait to find out who they were or what they wanted before opening his home. Generosity came first, and it came freely. That is the Quranic model: honour the guest because he is a guest, and leave the rest to Allah.

3.What Honouring a Guest Actually Means

So what does honouring a guest look like in an ordinary home, on an ordinary day? It begins before the food, with the welcome itself. A warm greeting, a face that shows pleasure rather than panic, a seat offered without delay — these cost nothing, and they are the first thing a guest remembers. The Prophet's own welcome was famously warm; visitors to his home were received with attention, not with the distracted half-presence of a person already thinking about the next task.

Then comes food and drink, offered promptly and without apology for its simplicity. Ibrahim's example is the guide: give the best you have, but do not delay generosity while chasing perfection. A simple meal served quickly and happily honours a guest far more than an elaborate meal served late and resentfully. And the host should eat with the guest where possible, so that the guest does not eat alone — a small courtesy that changes the whole feeling of the meal.

Honour also means comfort and privacy. A clean place to sit, a place to rest, a bathroom the guest can use without embarrassment, and — for an overnight guest — a proper place to sleep. It means not interrogating the guest about why he came, how long he will stay, or what he earns. It means protecting his dignity in your home the way you would protect your own. And it means seeing him off kindly when he leaves, with a prayer for his journey, not a sigh of relief.

4.The Guest's Manners Matter Too

Hospitality is a two-way sunnah, and the guest carries manners of his own. The first is gratitude: to accept what is offered graciously, to praise the food, and to pray for the host. The second is ease: to be the kind of guest who does not make his host's life difficult — arriving at a reasonable time, not demanding what the house cannot provide, and keeping his needs simple. The third is the limit the hadith sets: three days of hospitality, and beyond that, awareness that continued staying becomes a burden unless the host insists with genuine pleasure.

The Quran itself teaches the guest's side of the bargain in a verse about visiting the Prophet's household. After eating, the believers are told, in meaning, to disperse, without lingering for conversation (Quran 33:53). The verse was revealed for a specific household and a specific situation, but the principle it carries is universal: a guest should not overstay his welcome, however enjoyable the company. Good company knows when to leave, and leaving at the right time is itself a form of respect. Our article on good company explains why the people we spend time with shape our faith — and a good guest is good company in its purest form.

There is also a quieter guest manner that is easy to miss: do not compare. A guest who praises another host's house, comments on what is missing, or broadcasts the host's shortcomings afterwards has broken the trust of the visit. What is seen in someone's home stays in someone's home, unless sharing it would help. Hospitality is built on trust, and trust is kept by the guest just as much as by the host.

5.Hospitality Without Showing Off or Debt

One reason hospitality has faded is that it has become expensive — or rather, we have made it expensive. The fear of not measuring up keeps many doors closed: the house is not grand enough, the food is not fancy enough, the crockery does not match. Islam removes that fear entirely. The sunnah of hospitality is sincerity, not display. A host who borrows money to impress a guest has misunderstood the sunnah; a host who offers dates and water with a full heart has understood it perfectly.

The scholars have long warned against takalluf — burdening oneself with artificial formality for a guest. True honour is natural and unforced. It is the difference between a home where the guest feels like family and a home where the guest feels like an inspection. Children learn this by watching: when they see their parents welcome a visitor with joy rather than stress, they learn that people matter more than appearances. And when a guest is received simply, he leaves not with a memory of luxury, but with a memory of love — which is the only memory that matters.

The same simplicity protects the host's own worship. A visit should not become a reason to miss prayers, neglect family, or fall into debt. Generosity in Islam is always balanced: give freely, but do not harm yourself or your dependents in the giving. The middle path — warm, simple, sustainable — is the sunnah, and it is the only kind of hospitality a household can practise for a lifetime.

6.Bringing the Sunnah Back Into Your Home

Reviving this sunnah does not require a renovation or a feast. It requires a decision, and a few small habits. Invite someone this week — a relative you have not seen, a new neighbour, a student far from home, a friend going through a hard time. Keep the food simple and the welcome warm. Put the phone away when the guest is speaking. Ask about his life, not about his plans. Pray for him aloud before he leaves, the way hosts once did as a matter of course.

Make a corner of the home ready for guests: a clean seating place, a spare towel, a prayer mat within reach. Teach children to greet visitors, to offer water, to give up their seat. And remember the reward: the Prophet joined honouring the guest to belief in Allah and the Last Day, which means every guest at your door is an opportunity to prove, quietly and practically, what you believe. A home that welcomes guests is a home that practises its faith out loud.

The door of your house is, in a small way, the door of your heart. Open it with the warmth Ibrahim showed, the sincerity the Prophet taught, and the simplicity that keeps hospitality alive for a lifetime.
