Isra wal-Mi'raj: The Night Journey

R Razia Azeem · 13 Nov, 2026

In a single night, the Prophet ﷺ travelled from Makkah to Jerusalem and rose through seven heavens — and came back with the gift of five daily prayers.

Seerah & Stories

In one night, the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) travelled from Makkah to Jerusalem, prayed with the prophets, rose through seven heavens, and stood at the edge of creation itself — and was home before the dawn. It is the most extraordinary journey in the Seerah, and it came at the darkest moment of his life. The Night Journey, Al-Isra wal-Mi'raj, is a story about what Allah does when His servant has nothing left: He opens the heavens.

This article tells the story as the Quran and the authentic narrations tell it — what happened, what the Prophet (peace be upon him) saw, what he brought back, and what it means for the five prayers you and I pray every day.

1.The Darkest Year Before the Brightest Night

To understand the Night Journey, you have to know the year it came in. The tenth year of prophethood is known in the Seerah as the Year of Sorrow. In it, the Prophet (peace be upon him) lost the two people who had protected him from the start: his uncle Abu Talib, whose standing kept Quraysh from killing him, and his wife Khadijah (may Allah be pleased with her), his first believer and his comfort. Both died within months of each other. The protection was gone, and the grief was total.

Then came Ta'if. With Makkah closed against him, he walked to Ta'if hoping its people would listen. Instead they drove him out, and children pelted him with stones until his sandals filled with blood. He left with nothing — no new believers, no safe city, no protector left on earth — and prayed in that hour only that Allah would not be angry with him.

It was after this — in the eleventh year of his prophethood, around 621 CE, about a year before the Hijrah — that the Night Journey came. Allah did not send him an army, or a kingdom, or a victory in Makkah. He sent him a night — and in that night, showed him that the One who had never left him was greater than every loss. The Quran pairs hardship with ease more than once: "For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease. Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease" (Quran 94:5-6). The Mi'raj is that promise, lived out in a single night.

2.From Makkah to Jerusalem: The Night Journey

The Quran opens the story with a single verse that contains the whole journey's first half: "Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa, whose surroundings We have blessed, to show him of Our signs. Indeed, He is the Hearing, the Seeing" (Quran 17:1). Notice the words: Allah is the one who took him — the journey was Allah's act, not the Prophet's. He is called "His Servant", the highest title, given at the highest moment. And the purpose is stated plainly: "to show him of Our signs."

سُبْحَٰنَ ٱلَّذِىٓ أَسْرَىٰ بِعَبْدِهِۦ لَيْلًا مِّنَ ٱلْمَسْجِدِ ٱلْحَرَامِ إِلَى ٱلْمَسْجِدِ ٱلْأَقْصَا

Quran 17:1 — "Exalted is He who took His Servant by night from al-Masjid al-Haram to al-Masjid al-Aqsa…"

Al-Bukhari's account has the Prophet (peace be upon him) lying in al-Hijr, the low-walled enclosure beside the Kaaba, when Jibril came and his chest was opened and washed with Zamzam water (Sahih al-Bukhari 349). Then he was brought al-Buraq — a white animal, larger than a donkey but smaller than a mule, whose stride reached as far as its eye could see (Sahih Muslim 162a; Sahih al-Bukhari 3887). He mounted it, and in moments he was in Jerusalem.

There, at Bayt al-Maqdis, he tethered Buraq to the ring to which the prophets tethered their mounts, entered the mosque, and prayed two rak'ahs in it (Sahih Muslim 162a). Then Jibril offered him two vessels — milk and wine — and he chose the milk. "You have chosen the fitrah," Jibril said — the natural, pure disposition Allah created mankind upon. This was not a dream: the Prophet (peace be upon him) travelled, in body, from one mosque to another, and prayed there. The two mosques of the journey are the two ends of the Muslim world: the Haram in Makkah, where the Kaaba stands, and al-Aqsa in Jerusalem, the first qibla. Our article on the virtues of Masjid al-Aqsa explains why that second mosque holds such a place in our hearts.

3.Through the Seven Heavens

From Jerusalem, the ascent began. The fullest account is al-Bukhari's narration from Malik ibn Sa'sa'ah, who heard the Prophet (peace be upon him) tell it himself (Sahih al-Bukhari 349). At each heaven, Jibril asked for the gate to be opened, and the gatekeeper asked who was with him. "Muhammad," Jibril answered. "Has he been sent for?" "Yes." And the gates opened.

In each heaven, a prophet of Allah welcomed him: Adam in the first; Yahya and Isa in the second; Yusuf in the third; Idris in the fourth; Harun in the fifth; Musa in the sixth; and Ibrahim in the seventh, leaning against al-Bayt al-Ma'mur — the Frequented House, the heavenly counterpart of the Kaaba, which seventy thousand angels enter every day and never return to (Sahih Muslim 162a). Each greeted him as a righteous brother and a righteous prophet.

One greeting is remembered with particular tenderness. Passing Musa in the sixth heaven, the Prophet (peace be upon him) found him weeping — because a young man sent after him would have more followers entering Paradise than his own (Sahih al-Bukhari 349). A prophet of Allah weeping, not from envy but from love, glad the ummah after him would be so blessed. The heavens were not empty halls; they were full of brothers, each waiting to welcome him.

4.Sidrat al-Muntaha: The Lote Tree

The ascent ended at a tree. Sidrat al-Muntaha — the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary — is where everything rising from the earth stops, and everything descending begins. The Prophet (peace be upon him) described it in Sahih al-Bukhari 349: its fruits were like the great jars of Hajr, its leaves as big as the ears of elephants. Then it was covered by what covered it, and the narration does not try to describe what that was.

The Quran speaks of this moment in Surah an-Najm: "And he certainly saw him in another descent, at the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary — near it is the Garden of Refuge — when there covered the Lote Tree that which covered [it]. The sight [of the Prophet] did not swerve, nor did it transgress. He certainly saw of the greatest signs of his Lord" (Quran 53:13-18). The Quran's own words are the surest part of the story — everything else rests on them.

"He certainly saw of the greatest signs of his Lord." (Quran 53:18)

One honest question belongs here. Did the Prophet (peace be upon him) see Allah Himself on that night? Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) was asked this directly, and her answer is recorded in Sahih Muslim: he did not see his Lord with his eyes — the vision the Quran describes was of Jibril (Sahih Muslim 177a). Ibn Abbas (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that he saw Him with his heart (Sahih Muslim 176a). Scholars have differed on how to hold these reports together, and a careful reader should know the difference exists. What is certain is what the Quran itself says: he saw "of the greatest signs of his Lord" — and that is enough.

5.Fifty Prayers Became Five

At the highest point of the journey came its gift: Allah prescribed fifty prayers a day. On the way down, the Prophet (peace be upon him) passed Musa, who asked what had been ordered. "Fifty prayers a day," he answered. Musa said: your followers cannot bear fifty — I have tested people before you, and tried my hardest with the Children of Israel. Go back and ask Him to lighten it. So he went back, and Allah reduced the number by ten — and again, and again, fifty, forty, thirty, twenty, ten, until five remained. Musa urged him to go back once more, but he said he was too shy before his Lord to ask again. And Allah declared: the five are five in number, and fifty in reward — His decree would not change (Sahih al-Bukhari 349; Sahih Muslim 162a).

Think about what that means. Every prayer you have ever prayed — Fajr, Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha — was given on that night, in the heavens themselves. Scholars note that salah is the only act of worship given to the Prophet (peace be upon him) directly in the heavens, not revealed on earth. Every other command came down to him. Prayer went up to him — and he brought it back down to us.

That is why prayer is not a burden added to your day; it is the gift of the hardest night of the Prophet's life, carried back for you. The Quran commands us to guard it: "Maintain with care the [obligatory] prayers" (Quran 2:238). Our article on the virtue of salah, the pillar of the religion explains why those five prayers hold the whole building up. If you take one thing from the Night Journey, take this: he climbed to the heavens and came back with five prayers. The least we can do is pray them.

6.What the Night Journey Teaches Us Today

The first lesson is about truth. When the Prophet (peace be upon him) returned and told Quraysh what had happened, they laughed — a journey to Jerusalem and back in one night, when their own caravans took a month each way, was the perfect reason to mock him. They demanded proof: describe Jerusalem, since you claim you were there. And Allah displayed Jerusalem before him, so he described its features exactly as they stood (Sahih al-Bukhari 4710). The seerah reports that Abu Bakr (may Allah be pleased with him) believed at once, without asking for proof. That is why he is called As-Siddiq, "the Truthful One": faith that does not wait for the evidence to catch up.

The second lesson is about hope. The Mi'raj came at the lowest point of the Prophet's life — after the Year of Sorrow, after Ta'if, when every door on earth was closed — and it came as a night, not a victory. Allah's answer to the hardest year was not to remove the hardship, but to show His servant something greater than it. If you are in a hard year of your own, hold this promise: "For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease" (Quran 94:5-6). The ease may not look like the victory you imagined. It may look like a night, a prayer, a door you had not seen.

The third lesson is honesty about what we know and what we do not. Many Muslims commemorate the Night Journey on the 27th night of Rajab — a widespread tradition. But no authentic report establishes the exact date, and the scholars differ on it, so it should be described as a tradition, not a certainty. The event is certain; the date is not. A faith honest about that distinction is stronger, not weaker, for it.

And the last lesson is the practical one. The Prophet (peace be upon him) did not come back from the heavens with a new empire, or a new army, or a new city. He came back with five prayers — and, a year later, with the Hijrah, the journey that changed history. The heavens gave him prayer; the earth would give him a new home. Both began in the dark, and both began with trust. Tonight, when you pray Isha, remember where those prayers came from: a night when a grieving Prophet was carried past the stars and sent back with a gift for you. Pray them like a gift — because that is what they are.
