Sincerity (Ikhlas): Doing Good Only for Allah

R Razia Azeem · 28 Oct, 2026

Two people give the same charity. One gives quietly and walks away; the other gives where everyone can see, and waits for the praise. The money is the same. The deed is not. Islam calls the difference ikhlas — sincerity: doing good for Allah alone, with no second audience in mind.

Two people give the same amount to the same cause. One gives quietly and walks away, content that Allah saw it. The other gives where everyone can see, and waits — for the thanks, the reputation, the admiring comment. The money is the same. The deed is not. Islam gives the difference a name: ikhlas, sincerity. It means doing good for Allah alone, with no second audience sitting in the back of your mind.

Sincerity is not a decoration added to a good deed. It is the deed's soul. A prayer offered to impress, charity given to be praised, knowledge shared to be admired — all of these wear the shape of worship while missing its heart. And the opposite is just as striking: a small, hidden act done purely for Allah can outweigh a grand public one done for applause. That is why the scholars called ikhlas the hardest thing to get right and the most important thing to keep checking. You cannot see your own intention the way others see your actions, and it can change mid-deed without you noticing.

1. What Ikhlas Really Is

The word ikhlas comes from a root meaning to purify, to make something clear of mixture. Pure gold has nothing mixed into it; pure milk has nothing added to it. A sincere deed is like that: worship with nothing mixed in — no desire for praise, no hunger for status, no bargain with people's opinions. It is not that a sincere person never feels pleasure when someone appreciates him. Pleasure that arrives uninvited is not the problem. The problem is when the applause becomes the reason: when you would not have done the deed at all if no one had been watching.

Think of it as a question you can ask before any good act: "If absolutely no one ever knew about this — no post, no mention, no thanks — would I still do it, and do it just as well?" If the answer is yes, your intention is probably clean. If the answer is no, or if the quality of your effort would drop the moment the audience left, then something has been mixed into the deed. That honest question, asked often, is itself an act of sincerity.

Sincerity also means singleness of purpose across a whole life, not just in worship. A sincere merchant is honest when no inspector is watching. A sincere student studies to learn, not only to be called clever. A sincere parent is kind to his children when no guest is present to admire his parenting. Ikhlas turns every ordinary act into worship, because the same Lord is watching in every room.

2. The Quran's Command: Worship Purely for Him

The Quran states the command in the plainest possible words: "And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion, inclining to truth, and to establish prayer and to give zakah. And that is the correct religion" (Quran 98:5). Sincerity is not presented as an advanced extra for saints. It is the first thing the command lists — the very definition of the correct religion, placed before prayer and charity themselves. Prayer and charity without sincerity are bodies without souls.

The same demand appears at the opening of Surah Az-Zumar: "Indeed, We have sent down to you the Book in truth. So worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion" (Quran 39:2). And a verse later the reason is given: "Unquestionably, for Allah is the pure religion" (Quran 39:3, in meaning). Pure religion belongs to Allah alone. The moment a deed is offered partly to someone else's opinion — partly to a crowd, a reputation, a career — it is no longer pure, and it no longer fully belongs to Him.

"And they were not commanded except to worship Allah, [being] sincere to Him in religion, inclining to truth." — Quran 98:5

3. Intentions Decide Every Deed

The most famous hadith in all of Islam is also the first hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari. Umar ibn al-Khattab (RA) reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Actions are but by intentions, and every man shall have only that which he intended" (Sahih al-Bukhari 1). The same act, performed by two people with different intentions, earns two different rewards. The same act, performed by one person on two days with two different hearts, is two different deeds in the sight of Allah.

This is why intention matters more than scale. A small charity given with a pure heart is greater than a fortune given for fame. A quiet prayer at home, prayed only because Allah commanded it, is greater than a long public prayer performed to be admired. And the hadith cuts the other way too: an act that looks religious can earn nothing at all if its intention was worldly, while an ordinary act — eating, sleeping, working, resting — can earn reward if its intention is to gain strength for Allah's obedience. Intention is the currency; the outward act is only the coin it is stamped on.

The practical lesson is to pause before you act. The scholars used to say: before you speak, check your intention; before you give, check your intention; before you post, check your intention. That pause takes five seconds. It is the difference between a deed that lives forever in your record and a deed that evaporates the moment the applause fades.

4. The Hidden Danger: Showing Off

The opposite of sincerity has a name too: riya, showing off — doing good deeds so that people will see and praise them. The Quran warns about it in the sharpest terms. Describing those who pray carelessly and perform only to be seen, it says, in meaning: "So woe to those who pray, [but] who are heedless of their prayer — those who make show [of their deeds]" (Quran 107:4-6). The prayer itself is not condemned; the showing off is. A deed performed for the eyes of people is offered to the wrong audience, and the wrong audience cannot reward it on the Day when only Allah's judgement counts.

Charity is given as the clearest example. "O you who have believed, do not invalidate your charities with reminders or injury as does one who spends his wealth [only] to be seen by the people" (Quran 2:264, in meaning). Two things are named as invalidating charity: reminding the recipient of your favour, and spending to be seen. The first wounds the person you helped; the second wounds the deed itself. Both turn a gift into a transaction — and Allah, who needs nothing, accepts no transaction in which He is not the intended recipient.

And Allah's measure of worth is stated in a hadith that should humble every one of us. Abu Hurayrah (RA) reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Verily, Allah does not look at your appearance or wealth, but rather He looks at your hearts and actions" (Sahih Muslim 2564). Your audience on social media sees your appearance and your wealth. Allah sees something else entirely: the heart behind the action. Every deed is therefore judged twice — once by people, who see the outside, and once by Allah, who sees the inside. Only the second judgement lasts.

5. Sincerity Toward People, Too

Ikhlas is not only about worship; it is about honesty with people. Tamim ad-Dari (RA) reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Religion is sincerity." When asked, "To whom?" he replied: "To Allah, His Book, His Messenger, the leaders of the Muslims and to their common people" (Sahih Muslim 55, in meaning). Sincerity to Allah is worshipping Him alone. Sincerity to His Book is believing it and living by it. Sincerity to His Messenger ﷺ is following his way, not merely praising his name. Sincerity to leaders and to ordinary people is wanting good for them honestly — advising them truthfully, not flattering them for advantage, and not smiling to their faces while working against them behind their backs.

This widens sincerity from a private matter of the heart into a public matter of character. A sincere friend tells you the uncomfortable truth kindly, instead of the comfortable lie politely. A sincere colleague gives credit honestly instead of stealing it quietly. A sincere neighbour wishes for his neighbour what he wishes for himself — the same standard our article on loving for your brother explores. When sincerity becomes your way of dealing with people, your worship stops being a performance and starts being a life.

Notice, too, what sincerity is not. It is not bluntness for its own sake, and it is not announcing your good deeds to prove you have nothing to hide. A sincere person can keep a good deed secret, and a sincere person can also do a good deed openly — if the openness itself serves a good purpose, like encouraging others to give. The question is never "was anyone watching?" The question is always "for whom did you do it?"

6. Seven Practices That Purify Intentions

Like every virtue in our recent series — humility, contentment, gentleness — sincerity is trained, not wished for. These seven practices are small and daily. Pick one this week.

1. Pause for five seconds before a good deed. Name your intention silently: "I am doing this for Allah." That tiny pause catches more mixed motives than any lecture ever will.

2. Do one good deed every day that no one will ever know about. A secret charity, a prayer no one sees, help given without a witness. Hidden deeds are sincerity's training ground — they teach the heart to be content with Allah alone as its audience.

3. Delay the post. Before sharing a good act online, wait a day and ask: "Am I sharing this to encourage others, or to be admired?" If you cannot answer honestly, keep it between you and Allah.

4. Thank Allah before thanking the audience. When praise arrives, redirect it inwardly at once: "This was from Allah, not from me." Gratitude for the ability to do good protects the deed from becoming a trophy.

5. Renew intentions in the middle, not just at the start. Intentions drift mid-deed — a prayer that began sincerely can end in showing off. When you notice the drift, quietly return: "O Allah, I am doing this for You." Returning is not failure; it is the practice itself.

6. Accept being misunderstood. Sometimes sincerity means doing the right thing while people think the worst of you. If you need everyone's good opinion, your deeds will always belong to them. Letting go of that need is freedom.

7. Ask Allah for sincerity itself. The heart is not fully in your control — it turns, and only Allah steadies it. Make it a daily dua: "O Allah, make my deeds sincere for Your sake alone." Asking for ikhlas is already a sign of it.

7. One Deed, One Audience

At the end of the day, sincerity simplifies everything. You do not need to manage ten audiences — your family, your friends, your followers, your rivals — each with a different performance. You need one audience, and He already sees you. He saw the charity you gave in secret. He saw the prayer you prayed when you were tired and no one was watching. He saw the kind word you said to someone who could do nothing for you. None of it was wasted, because none of it was performed for anyone but Him.

Try the five-second pause tomorrow, before the first good deed of your day. Name the intention, do the deed, and let the praise — or the silence — be what it is. A deed done for Allah alone is never small, never wasted, and never forgotten. And a heart trained to seek only His face will find, in this world and the next, that one audience was always enough.
