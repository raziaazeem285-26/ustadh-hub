Courage (Shaja'ah): Standing Firm When Fear Says Run

R Razia Azeem · 29 Oct, 2026

Fear is not the opposite of faith. Every human being feels it — before a difficult conversation, in front of a crowd, when a diagnosis arrives, when doing the right thing will cost you. Courage in Islam is not the absence of that feeling. It is what you do while feeling it: you stand firm, you speak the truth, you trust Allah, and you do not run from what is right.

Ask a child what a brave person looks like and he will probably describe someone who feels no fear at all — a hero who walks into danger smiling. Islam gives a truer picture. The brave person feels fear, just like everyone else, and still does what is right. His knees may shake, his voice may tremble, but his feet do not move backwards. That is shaja'ah, and it belongs in the same company as the virtues this blog has been exploring — justice, mercy, truthfulness — because without courage, the other virtues stay only in the heart and never reach the world.

Think of it plainly. Truthfulness takes courage when the truth will embarrass you. Justice takes courage when fairness favours the other side. Mercy takes courage when everyone around you is calling for revenge. A virtue that is never tested by fear is only a nice idea. Courage is the virtue that carries the rest of them out of the house and into real life.

1. What Courage Really Is

The Arabic word shaja'ah means bravery, but the Islamic understanding of it is wider than battlefield bravery. It is firmness of heart in the face of anything that frightens you: a powerful person, a hostile crowd, a painful truth, a loss you cannot undo. It is the opposite of two different failures. One is recklessness — rushing into danger for show, without wisdom or need. The other is cowardice — abandoning what is right because you are afraid of the cost. True courage sits between them: you see the danger clearly, you count the cost honestly, and you still do what Allah asks, trusting Him with the outcome.

That is why courage in Islam is never separated from trust in Allah. A person who trusts only himself will be brave only while he feels strong, and will collapse the day his strength runs out. A person who trusts Allah can be brave when he is weak, poor, outnumbered and afraid — because his courage does not come from his own power. It comes from knowing who is really in charge.

2. Hasbunallahu: The Words of the Brave

The Quran gives us the very words of brave people, so that we can borrow them when our own words fail. After the Battle of Uhud, when the Muslims were wounded and exhausted, word reached them that the enemy army had gathered against them again. People said to them: fear them. The Quran records their answer: “It [merely] increased them in faith, and they said, ‘Sufficient for us is Allah, and [He is] the best Disposer of affairs’” (Quran 3:173, in meaning). Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel — Allah is enough for us, and He is the best One to trust with our affairs.

Notice what the verse does not say. It does not say the believers stopped feeling fear, or that the army disappeared, or that the danger was imaginary. The threat was real, the wounds were real, and still their faith increased. That is the pattern of courage in the Quran: fear arrives, faith answers louder. The same words, Ibn Abbas (RA) reported, were said by Ibrahim (AS) when he was thrown into the fire, and by Muhammad ﷺ when he was told that an army had gathered against him (Sahih al-Bukhari, in meaning). When you say Hasbunallahu, you are not reciting a magic formula. You are joining a line of brave people, from Ibrahim to the Companions, who faced fire and armies with the same sentence on their lips.

3. Do Not Weaken, Do Not Grieve

Two other verses frame courage as a command, not a personality trait. “So do not weaken and do not grieve, and you will be superior if you are [true] believers” (Quran 3:139, in meaning). And: “Say, ‘Never will we be struck except by what Allah has decreed for us; He is our protector.’ And upon Allah let the believers rely” (Quran 9:51, in meaning). Read together, they answer the two thoughts that make cowards of us. The first thought is: I am too weak for this. The answer is: do not weaken — your strength was never meant to come from you alone. The second thought is: what if the worst happens? The answer is: nothing will strike you except what Allah has already decreed, and what He decrees for a believer is never without wisdom and reward.

The Quran gives the same lesson in the story of Talut's army: “How many a small company has overcome a large company by permission of Allah. And Allah is with the patient” (Quran 2:249, in meaning). Numbers do not decide outcomes. Allah does. A small group that trusts Him can stand where a large group that trusts only itself will run. That is why a Muslim does not measure a cause by counting supporters before deciding whether to stand for it. He measures it by whether it is right, and then he stands, whether he stands alone or with thousands.

4. The Bravest of All People

Anas ibn Malik (RA) described the Prophet ﷺ in three words that belong together: “The Prophet was the best, the bravest and the most generous of all the people” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6033, in meaning). The same narration tells us what that bravery looked like on an ordinary night. The people of Madinah were frightened by a loud sound in the night, and they rushed towards it — and they met the Prophet ﷺ already coming back, having ridden out ahead of them alone on a horse without a saddle, a sword slung at his neck, saying, “Do not be afraid, do not be afraid.” He did not send someone else to check. He did not wait for a guard. The leader of the community was the first one out of the door and the first one back with reassurance.

The same steadfastness showed at Hunayn. The Quran describes that day plainly: the Muslims were pleased with their large numbers, “but it availed you nothing, and the earth, for all its breadth, was straitened for you, then you turned back, retreating” (Quran 9:25, in meaning). In the confusion, the Prophet ﷺ stood firm and called out, in meaning, “I am the Prophet, no lie; I am the son of Abdul-Muttalib” — announcing who he was to the enemy at the very moment when hiding would have been safer. His steadfastness gathered the scattered Companions back around him, and the battle turned. Courage, in that moment, was not a private feeling. It was a public act that gave other people something firm to rally to. That is how courage usually works: one person standing still gives ten frightened people permission to stand still too.

5. Seeking Refuge from Cowardice

It is telling that the Prophet ﷺ regularly sought refuge with Allah from cowardice, just as he sought refuge from miserliness, laziness and the punishment of the grave. Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas (RA) taught his sons the words as a teacher teaches writing: “O Allah! I seek refuge with You from cowardice, and seek refuge with You from being brought back to a bad stage of old life, and seek refuge with You from the afflictions of the world, and seek refuge with You from the punishments in the grave” (Sahih al-Bukhari 2822, in meaning). A similar supplication is reported in Sahih al-Bukhari 6390, again joining cowardice with miserliness.

Why would the bravest of people need to seek refuge from cowardice? Because courage is not a permanent possession. It is a daily gift, and it can leak away quietly — through comfort, through fear of what people will say, through the slow habit of choosing the easy silence over the hard truth. The dua teaches us two things. First, cowardice is a real spiritual danger, not a minor flaw; it can make a person abandon a duty, betray a trust, or stay silent while wrong is done. Second, the cure for it is not self-help slogans but turning to Allah: admit the fear to Him, and ask Him for the firmness you do not have on your own.

The dua does not ask Allah to remove all fear. Fear is a human alarm — useful when it tells you to prepare and be careful. Cowardice is different: it is fear winning the argument and making your decisions for you. A brave person still hears the alarm; he just does not obey it when it tells him to abandon what is right.

6. Courage in Ordinary Life

Most of us will never face an army, and that is a mercy. But courage is not waiting for a battlefield. It is needed in rooms far smaller and quieter. It takes courage to tell a friend, kindly, that what he is doing is wrong — and to keep his friendship while doing it. It takes courage to apologise first, when apologising makes you look weak. It takes courage to refuse a profitable deal that involves dishonesty, to defend someone who is being mocked in a group chat, to admit a mistake at work before anyone discovers it, to keep praying and wearing your identity openly when the people around you treat faith as something embarrassing.

Parents know a quieter form of courage too: the courage to say no to a child you love, to hold a boundary when holding it makes you unpopular for an evening, to teach your children the Quran and the Sunnah when the world around them teaches the opposite with far louder voices. And patients know another: the courage to face an illness or a loss without losing faith, to say Hasbunallahu in a hospital room the way the Companions said it before an army. None of these moments will make the news. All of them are shaja'ah, because in each one fear said run, and the person stayed.

7. Five Daily Ways to Practise Courage

Courage, like any virtue, is trained in small repetitions before it is needed in large ones. These five practices build it day by day.

1. Say Hasbunallahu out loud when fear arrives. Do not only think it. Say it, slowly, before the difficult phone call, the honest conversation, the decision you have been postponing. The words connect your small fear to Ibrahim's fire and the Companions' army, and that connection itself steadies the heart.

2. Do one hard truth a day. Tell the truth about a small thing when a comfortable half-truth would be easier — admit the error, correct the exaggeration, give credit where it is due. Small truths are training weights for big ones.

3. Seek refuge from cowardice in your duas. Add the Prophet's words — “O Allah, I seek refuge with You from cowardice” — to your daily supplications, and mean them. Naming the weakness to Allah is the first step to being freed from it.

4. Prepare before you need courage. Decide your lines in advance: what you will say when gossip starts, what you will refuse when the dishonest offer comes, how you will answer when faith is mocked. People who decide in calm moments do not have to improvise in frightened ones.

5. Stand beside someone else. Courage grows fastest when it is shared. Defend the person being mocked, sit with the person who is grieving, accompany the person who must deliver bad news. You will find that standing beside someone else makes your own legs steadier — and your steadiness does the same for them.

Fear will come again tomorrow, in some new shape. That is not a failure of faith; it is the condition in which faith is exercised. The question is never whether you will feel afraid. The question is whether, feeling afraid, you will say what the brave have always said — Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel — and then stand firm.
