Farewell Sermon: The Prophet’s ﷺ Last Message

R Razia Azeem · 6 Nov, 2026

On a Friday, on the plain of Arafat, more than a hundred thousand Muslims gathered to hear the Prophet ﷺ speak for the last time. What he said that day was not a new teaching — it was the whole of his message, gathered into one final sermon: life is sacred, no one is above another except by taqwa, women are a trust from Allah, and the Book of Allah is the rope to hold.

Today is Friday. And it was on a Friday — the 9th of Dhul-Hijjah, in the 10th year after the Hijrah — that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ stood on the plain of Arafat and gave the sermon that history remembers as the Farewell Sermon, Khutbat al-Wada. It was his first and only Hajj, and within about three months he ﷺ would pass away. He knew his time with his people was ending, and he used it to gather twenty-three years of teaching into a few clear sentences that every Muslim, in every century since, has been asked to live by.

One honest note before we begin: the sermon as it circulates today is not one single speech copied from one page. The Prophet ﷺ spoke several times during those days of Hajj — at Arafat on the 9th, at Mina on the Day of Sacrifice, and again in the days that followed — and the companions preserved his words in different narrations. This article quotes each teaching with its own source, so you know exactly where every sentence comes from. Nothing here is stitched together and presented as a single quote.

1.A Friday at Arafat

The scene is described in the long narration of Jabir ibn Abdullah (may Allah be pleased with him) about the Prophet’s ﷺ pilgrimage, preserved in Sahih Muslim (1218). When the sun had passed its height, the Prophet ﷺ called for his camel al-Qaswa, rode down into the valley of Uranah at the edge of Arafat, and addressed the pilgrims from the saddle. His voice could not reach them all — more than a hundred thousand people had gathered — so Rabi’ah ibn Umayyah stood below him and repeated each sentence to those out of earshot.

He began, as the early sirah records, with words that must have silenced the whole plain: “O people, listen to my words, for I do not know whether I shall meet you again in this place after this year.” It was not a speech of triumph. The conquest of Makkah was behind him, Arabia had entered Islam, and the man who had once been driven out of his own city was now speaking to a crowd larger than any he had ever addressed. What he chose to say in that moment — knowing it might be the last time — tells us what mattered most to him.

It was during this pilgrimage, at Arafat, that a verse came down which the companions recognised as the completion of the message:

ٱلْيَوْمَ أَكْمَلْتُ لَكُمْ دِينَكُمْ وَأَتْمَمْتُ عَلَيْكُمْ نِعْمَتِي وَرَضِيتُ لَكُمُ ٱلْإِسْلَـٰمَ دِينًا

Quran 5:3 — “This day I have perfected for you your religion and completed My favour upon you and have approved for you Islam as religion.”

The religion was complete. What remained was for the people to hear it, hold it, and carry it to those who were not there.

2.“Your blood and your property are sacred”

The first teaching of the sermon, in Jabir’s narration in Sahih Muslim (1218), is the one the Prophet ﷺ repeated at Arafat and again at Mina:

“Verily your blood, your property are as sacred and inviolable as the sacredness of this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this town of yours.”

He measured the sacredness of a person’s life and property against three things every Arab in that crowd already held sacred beyond argument: the Day of Arafat, the month of Dhul-Hijjah, and the city of Makkah. A Muslim’s life, his wealth, and his honour are not less sacred than those — they are as sacred as them. And he added the reason: “You will surely meet your Lord, and He will ask you about your deeds.”

At Mina, as reported in Sahih al-Bukhari (67), he said it once more, and told the crowd to pass the message on: let those who are present convey it to those who are absent. The sermon was never meant to stay in that valley. It was meant to travel — and it has, for fourteen centuries, to us.

This is the foundation of everything else in the sermon. Before law, before worship, before politics, a Muslim must understand that another human being’s life and property are not his to take, damage, or play with — not by violence, not by fraud, not by a careless word that destroys a reputation. The man who truly believes he will stand before Allah and be asked about his deeds does not need a policeman to stop him from harming his neighbour.

3.The old order is finished

Then the Prophet ﷺ did something no conqueror in history had done: he cancelled the debts of his own family first. In the Arafat address (Sahih Muslim 1218), he declared that the blood-revenge of the Days of Ignorance was abolished, beginning with the blood of a member of his own family — and that the usury of the pre-Islamic period was abolished, beginning with the interest owed to his uncle al-Abbas. “Your capital belongs to you,” he said. “Wrong not, and you shall not be wronged.”

The Quran had already given the same rule: “Deal not unjustly, and you shall not be dealt with unjustly” (Quran 2:279, in meaning). The sermon made it a public, witnessed fact: the old system of inherited feuds and money growing on money was over, and the new order began with the Prophet’s ﷺ own household, not with his enemies.

There is a lesson in the order of it. Real reform does not start by pointing at others. It starts at home. A community that wants to end exploitation begins by cleaning its own dealings — its debts, its business, its family disputes — and only then has the right to ask others to change. 4.“Fear Allah concerning women”

Among the most quoted sentences of the sermon is the one about women. In Sahih Muslim (1218), the Prophet ﷺ said: “Fear Allah concerning women, for you have taken them as a trust from Allah.” A trust — an amanah — is not a possession. It is something placed in your hands that you will be asked about, and that you must return in a better state than you received it.

The Quran gives the same command in plain words: “Live with them in kindness” (Quran 4:19, in meaning). Kindness here is not a favour a husband grants when he is in a good mood; it is the standing order of the marriage itself. The Prophet ﷺ, who said in the sermon that women are a trust, lived it at home — mending his own clothes, helping with the housework, and never raising a hand against a wife or a servant, as his wife Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) described.

Notice where this teaching sits in the sermon: between the abolition of old injustices and the declaration of equality. It is not a side note. A society that claims to honour the sacredness of life while its women live in fear has not understood the sermon at all. The test of the Farewell Sermon is not how loudly we quote it on Jumu’ah — it is how a woman is treated in the home of the man who quotes it.

5.One family, one measure

Then came the sentence that has echoed through every century since, reported in Musnad Ahmad: “All mankind is from Adam and Eve. An Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab; a white person has no superiority over a black person, nor a black person over a white person — except by piety and good action.”

The Quran states the same measure: “The most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is the most God-fearing of you” (Quran 49:13, in meaning):

إِنَّ أَكْرَمَكُمْ عِندَ ٱللَّهِ أَتْقَىٰكُمْ

Quran 49:13 — “Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you.”

This was not an abstract idea for that crowd. Standing in it was Bilal ibn Rabah (may Allah be pleased with him) — a former slave, a Black man from Abyssinia, who had been tortured for his faith and freed by Abu Bakr — and it was his voice that had called the adhan over Makkah itself. Read his story in Hazrat Bilal (RA): The Voice of Faith That Torture Could Not Silence. The sermon’s words about equality were already visible in the Prophet’s ﷺ own community: the measure of a person was never his tribe, his colour, or his wealth. It was always his taqwa.

That measure still judges us. Every time we look down on someone for their race, their nationality, their accent, or their poverty, we are standing against the Farewell Sermon. And every time we honour someone only for their piety and character, we are standing with it.

6.What he left behind

At the end of the Arafat address, the Prophet ﷺ gave his people the one thing that would outlast him: “I have left among you the Book of Allah, and if you hold fast to it, you would never go astray” (Sahih Muslim 1218, in meaning). Then he raised his forefinger towards the sky, pointed it at the people, and said three times: “O Allah, bear witness.”

He had conveyed the message. The question the sermon leaves with us is whether we have received it. Allah describes the Prophet ﷺ as the pattern we are meant to follow: “There has certainly been for you in the Messenger of Allah an excellent pattern for anyone whose hope is in Allah and the Last Day” (Quran 33:21, in meaning). Following him ﷺ does not mean memorising the sermon; it means living it — guarding the life and property of others, ending the injustices we control, treating women as a trust, and measuring people only by their taqwa. For a reflection on what following him looks like day by day, read Loving the Prophet ﷺ: Love Shown by Following His Sunnah.

This Friday, take ten minutes and read the sermon again — slowly, as if you were standing in that crowd. Then choose one teaching from it and put it into practice this week: return something you owe, settle a debt fairly, speak kindly at home, or refuse one small act of prejudice. The Prophet ﷺ asked the people at Arafat, “Have I conveyed the message?” They answered, “Yes.” Our answer is not given in words. It is given in how we live.
