Forgiveness (Afw): The Strength That Frees Your Heart

R Razia Azeem · 26 Oct, 2026

Someone hurt you, and everyone says “just forgive and forget.” It sounds easy from the outside — and almost impossible from the inside. Islam does not ask you to pretend the hurt never happened. It calls forgiveness afw: a deliberate choice to release the debt, made not because the other person deserved it, but because your heart deserves peace, and because Allah promises the pardoner a reward with Him.

Think of the last time someone wronged you — a harsh word, a broken promise, a friend who disappeared when you needed them. Weeks later, the event is over, but the replay continues in your head. Islam names the way out of that loop: afw, forgiveness. Not forgetting. Not approval. A choice.

1. What Forgiveness Is — and Is Not

Forgiveness is not saying “what you did was fine.” It is not trusting the same person with the same thing again, and it is not staying in a harmful situation. The Quran itself pairs forgiveness with wisdom: “Take what is given freely, enjoin what is good, and turn away from the ignorant” (Quran 7:199). Turning away is sometimes part of forgiving — you release the grudge without reopening the door.

2. The Quran's Promise to the Pardoner

Allah describes the believers who earn His love like this: “those who restrain anger and who pardon the people — and Allah loves the doers of good” (Quran 3:134). Restraining anger is the first discipline; pardoning people is the second.

Then comes the promise itself: “The recompense for an evil is an evil like thereof, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is [due] from Allah” (Quran 42:40). Your pardon is not lost. It is not weakness that the world forgets. It is a deposit with Allah, and He does not lose deposits.

“Whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is [due] from Allah.” — Quran 42:40

A hadith in Sahih Muslim carries the same message into everyday life. Abu Hurairah (RA) reported that the Prophet  · said: “No one forgives except that Allah increases him in honour” (Sahih Muslim 2588). Forgiveness is not losing status; it is gaining honour from the only source whose honour lasts.

3. Yusuf and Abu Bakr: Forgiveness in Real Lives

The Quran gives us forgiveness not as an abstract virtue but as a scene. Yusuf (AS) was thrown into a well by his own brothers, sold as a slave, and imprisoned for a crime he did not commit. Years later, when those same brothers stood before him in Egypt, powerless and afraid, he said: “No blame will there be upon you today. Allah will forgive you; and He is the most merciful of the merciful” (Quran 12:92). He named the pardon and moved to reconciliation.

Abu Bakr (RA) faced a closer wound. Mistah, a relative he had supported financially, took part in spreading the slander against Aisha (RA). Hurt, Abu Bakr swore he would no longer help him. Then came the verse: “Let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?” (Quran 24:22). Abu Bakr answered: “Yes, by Allah, I would love that Allah forgive me” — and he resumed his support. That question is the heart of the subject: every one of us needs a pardon far greater than any pardon we are asked to give.

4. The Prophet's Pardon at Taif and Makkah

The Prophet  · lived forgiveness at its hardest. At Taif, after being mocked and driven out, he was offered — according to the well-known narration in Sahih al-Bukhari 3231 and Sahih Muslim 1795 — the destruction of the town by the angel of the mountains. He refused, hoping instead that Allah would bring forth from their descendants people who worship Allah alone.

At the Conquest of Makkah, the city that had persecuted and expelled him lay open before him. According to the seerah accounts, he announced a general pardon: “Go, you are free.” If he could pardon that, what is our excuse for nursing this? Our article on controlling anger covers the first step — restraining anger before forgiveness can follow.

5. Why Forgiving Frees You First

Here is the honest truth about grudges: the person you refuse to forgive is usually living their life, while you are the one replaying the scene at 2 a.m. A grudge is a debt you keep collecting in your own heart, from a debtor who may never pay. Forgiveness cancels the collection: it does not say the other person was right; it says you refuse to let them rent space in your head any longer.

6. Five Steps to Practise Afw

1. Name it honestly. Say what happened, without exaggerating or minimising. You cannot forgive a wound you refuse to look at.

2. Separate the person from the debt. You may never trust them in the same way again. That boundary is wisdom, not revenge; forgiveness releases the bitterness, not the boundary.

3. Make the dua before the feeling. Say: “O Allah, I pardon them for Your sake; pardon me.” The feeling often follows the decision, not the other way round.

4. Stop rehearsing the case. Every time the scene replays, refuse to prosecute it again. Repetition is how grudges stay alive; starving the replay is how they die.

5. Ask for Allah's pardon for yourself. Remember Quran 24:22. The fastest route to a forgiving heart is remembering how much you yourself need to be forgiven.

Forgiveness is not always a single moment. Sometimes it is a decision you remake each morning, until one morning the weight is gone. That is not failure; that is practice. And every time you choose it, your reward is with Allah. May He make us people who pardon easily and are pardoned generously. Ameen.
