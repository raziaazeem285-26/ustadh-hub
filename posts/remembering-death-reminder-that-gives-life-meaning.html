Remembering Death: The Reminder That Gives Life Meaning

R Razia Azeem · 22 Nov, 2026

Every soul shall taste death — and remembering it is not meant to frighten you, but to free you. What the Quran and Hadith teach about death, the grave, and living like a traveller.

In this article
  1. Every Soul Shall Taste Death
  2. The Destroyer of Pleasures
  3. Be a Stranger, Be a Traveller
  4. The Grave Is the First Stage
  5. Why Remembering Death Does Not Make Life Gloomy

Daily Life

There is one appointment no one can cancel, postpone, or negotiate. It does not ask for your schedule, your age, or your health. Every day it draws closer by exactly one day, whether you think about it or not. That appointment is death — and Islam does not ask you to hide from the thought of it. It asks you to remember it, often.

That sounds strange to a modern ear. We are taught to avoid the subject and treat death as a failure of medicine rather than a fact of life. But the Prophet ﷺ taught the opposite: remembering death is not a dark habit — it is the habit that gives life its colour. A person who remembers death stops wasting his days, forgives faster, repents sooner, and holds the world more loosely.

1.Every Soul Shall Taste Death

The Quran states the fact of death in the plainest possible words. In Surah Aal-e-Imran:

كُلُّ نَفۡسࣲ ذَاۤىِٕقَةُ ٱلۡمَوۡتِۗ وَإِنَّمَا تُوَفَّوۡنَ أُجُورَكُمۡ یَوۡمَ ٱلۡقِیَـٰمَةِۖ فَمَن زُحۡزِحَ عَنِ ٱلنَّارِ وَأُدۡخِلَ ٱلۡجَنَّةَ فَقَدۡ فَازَۗ وَمَا ٱلۡحَیَوٰةُ ٱلدُّنۡیَاۤ إِلَّا مَتَـٰعُ ٱلۡغُرُورِ

Quran 3:185

"Every soul shall taste death, and you will only be given your [full] compensation on the Day of Resurrection. So whoever is drawn away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise has attained [his desire]. And what is the life of this world except the enjoyment of delusion."

The verse announces the certainty of death, points beyond it to the Day of Resurrection, and names this world for what it is: mata' al-ghurur, the enjoyment of delusion. Nothing here is permanent, and nothing here is the final payment.

The same opening line appears again in Surah Al-Ankabut:

كُلُّ نَفۡسࣲ ذَاۤىِٕقَةُ ٱلۡمَوۡتِۖ ثُمَّ إِلَیۡنَا تُرۡجَعُونَ

Quran 29:57

"Every soul shall taste death. Then to Us you will be returned." The return is the point: death is not an end but a doorway, and on the other side is the One who gave life in the first place. And in Surah Al-Jumu'ah: "Say: Indeed, the death from which you flee — indeed, it will meet you" (Quran 62:8). You cannot outrun it; you can only prepare for it. The Quran also names the disease that makes us forget: "Competition for more distracts you, until you visit the graves" (Quran 102:1-2).

2.The Destroyer of Pleasures

The Prophet ﷺ gave remembering death a name that stays with you. Abu Hurairah (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: أَكْثِرُوا ذِكْرَ هَاذِمِ اللَّذَّاتِ — "Increase in remembrance of the severer of pleasures. Meaning death" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2307, graded Hasan).

Death is called the severer of pleasures because it cuts them off: every pleasure of this world ends, and death is the moment the last one ends. The Prophet ﷺ did not say "think about death once in a while." He said increase in remembering it — make it frequent, and let it sit beside you in your daily life.

Why would remembering something so final help a living person? Because pleasures are not the problem — attachment to them is. A pleasure enjoyed as a gift from Allah is a blessing; a pleasure chased as if it will last forever becomes a chain. Remembering death loosens the chain: in hardship it widens the heart, because the hardship will end; in ease it narrows the heart's greed, because the ease will end too.

Notice what the hadith does not say. It does not say "fear death" or "expect death tomorrow." It says remember it, the way a traveller keeps his destination in mind.

3.Be a Stranger, Be a Traveller

The Prophet ﷺ once taught this lesson with his hand on a shoulder. Abdullah ibn Umar (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated: "Allah's Messenger ﷺ took hold of my shoulder and said, 'Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler'" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6416).

The Arabic is short enough to memorise: كُنْ فِي الدُّنْيَا كَأَنَّكَ غَرِيبٌ، أَوْ عَابِرُ سَبِيلٍ. A stranger does not compete with a town's people for houses and honours; he is passing through. A traveller does not load himself with furniture; he carries only what he needs to reach the end.

Ibn Umar himself lived the lesson, and his words have become a daily rule for Muslims ever since: "If you survive till the evening, do not expect to be alive in the morning, and if you survive till the morning, do not expect to be alive in the evening, and take from your health for your sickness, and from your life for your death" (Sahih al-Bukhari 6416). He did not mean that you should expect to die tonight. He meant: do not postpone today's good deed to tomorrow, because tomorrow is not promised to you. Umar ibn al-Khattab (may Allah be pleased with him) is reported to have said, "Death is enough as an admonition." The simple fact that you will die, and you do not know when, is enough to wake a willing heart.

4.The Grave Is the First Stage

Death is not the last chapter. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the grave is the first stage of what comes after it. Hani, the freed slave of Uthman ibn Affan (may Allah be pleased with him), said that when Uthman would stop at a grave, he would weep until his beard was soaked. Asked why he wept at a grave but not at the mention of Paradise and the Fire, he replied: "Indeed, the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: 'Indeed the grave is the first stage among the stages of the Hereafter. So if one is saved from it, then what comes after it is easier than it. And if one is not saved from it, then what comes after it is worse than it'" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2308, graded Hasan).

Uthman was a man promised Paradise, and still he wept at graves — because the grave is the first test, and it decides the shape of everything after it. The Prophet ﷺ added: "I have not seen any sight except that the grave is more horrible than it" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2308). That is not said to terrify believers into despair, but to move them to prepare — because preparation is possible now, and only now.

What prepares a person for the grave is not wealth, family, or reputation; none of those enter it with him. Faith, prayer, charity, good character, and a heart that returns to Allah are the provision for that journey. That is why visiting graves is a Sunnah: standing at a grave and remembering that its occupant was once busy, healthy, and full of plans — just like you — puts your own plans in perspective in a minute.

5.Why Remembering Death Does Not Make Life Gloomy

Some worry that thinking about death will make life sad. The experience of the righteous is the opposite: remembering death makes life brighter, because it makes it more honest. A person who knows his time is limited stops spending it on quarrels that do not matter and grudges that poison his own heart. He spends it instead on what will still matter after he is gone: his relationship with Allah, his family, the good he leaves behind.

The Prophet ﷺ gave a practical version of this teaching: "Take advantage of five things before five things: your youth before your old age, your health before your sickness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your busy time, and your life before your death" (al-Hakim, al-Mustadrak 7846, graded Sahih). Each pair is a window that closes — an invitation to use it while it is open.

"Every soul shall taste death, and you will only be given your [full] compensation on the Day of Resurrection. So whoever is drawn away from the Fire and admitted to Paradise has attained [his desire]." — Quran 3:185

There is another mercy here. The door of repentance stays open for as long as a person is alive: the Prophet ﷺ said Allah accepts the repentance of His servant as long as the death-rattle has not reached his throat (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3537, graded Hasan). Whatever you have done, the way back is open right now. Remembering death does not close the door — it tells you to walk through it before it closes on its own.

6.What Death Changes Today

If you truly believed you might not see tomorrow, what would you do differently today? That question is the whole point — not to frighten you, but to focus you. Prayer becomes urgent: the prayer you were going to make up later gets prayed on time, because "later" is not guaranteed. Grudges become expensive: the argument you were nursing suddenly looks small against eternity. Money becomes lighter: the wealth you were hoarding for a future you may not see starts to flow — to parents and to the poor.

Sins become harder to enjoy, because the pleasure shrinks when you remember you may meet Allah tonight. Good deeds gain weight: the small, hidden deed — a prayer in the night, a charity no one sees — is the only currency that crosses the grave with you. The tongue slows down, and the heart turns homeward: the world stops looking like a permanent address and starts looking like what it is — a road, a field where you plant for a harvest you will gather somewhere else.

7.Seven Ways to Remember Death Well

Remembering death is a practice, not a mood. Here are seven simple ways to build it into your life, each drawn from the Sunnah or the practice of the righteous.

1. Read Surah Aal-e-Imran 3:185 once a week. Read it slowly, in Arabic if you can and in translation if you cannot. A verse read every week becomes a compass.

2. Visit a graveyard. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Visit the graves, for they remind you of the Hereafter" (Sahih Muslim 976). Stand quietly, make dua for them, and remember they were once as busy as you are. Ten minutes resets a whole week.

3. Attend a janazah when you can. Praying the funeral prayer is a right of the Muslim over his brother — and one of the most honest classrooms in the world.

4. Shorten your hopes each morning. Say to yourself what Ibn Umar said: if I reach the evening, I may not reach the morning. Then ask: what is the one good thing I must do today? Do it first.

5. Settle your debts and your grudges. Debts follow a person even after death. Pay what you owe, ask forgiveness where you wronged someone, and forgive where you were wronged.

6. Write your will. The Prophet ﷺ said a Muslim should not let two nights pass with something to bequeath without his will written (Sahih al-Bukhari 2738; Sahih Muslim 1627). It protects your family from disputes after you.

7. End each day with istighfar. Before sleep, ask Allah's forgiveness for the day — its mistakes and wasted hours. The one who ends his day in repentance sleeps like a traveller who has checked his bags. If the night is his last, he has already made his peace.

Death is coming to all of us — young and old, healthy and sick, rich and poor. The question is whether we will meet it prepared, with a heart softened by remembering it, or surprised, with a heart that spent its life looking the other way. Remember it often, and live like a traveller.
