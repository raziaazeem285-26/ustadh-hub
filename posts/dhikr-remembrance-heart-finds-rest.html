Dhikr: How Remembering Allah Brings the Heart to Rest

R Razia Azeem · 31 Oct, 2026

Modern life gives the heart a thousand things to chase and almost nothing to rest on. News, notifications, worries about money, health and family — the mind is never quiet, and the heart is never still. Fourteen centuries ago, the Quran named the one thing that actually brings the heart to rest, and it is not a place, a purchase or a person. It is dhikr: remembering Allah.

Ask a worried person what would calm them, and they will usually name something outside themselves: a holiday, a sum of money, a problem solved. Islam points in the opposite direction. The Quran says, in the meaning of Quran 13:28, that it is in the remembrance of Allah that hearts find rest. Not in having everything sorted. Not in escaping life. In remembering the One who holds it all.

Dhikr is the simplest worship in Islam — and, in a busy life, often the most neglected. It needs no wudu in its simplest form, no special place, no money and no audience. A person can do it walking to work, waiting in a queue, cooking a meal, or lying awake at night. Yet this small practice is described as the difference between a living heart and a dead one. This article explains what dhikr truly is, what Allah promises those who practise it, and how to weave it into an ordinary day.

1.What Dhikr Really Is

Most people hear the word dhikr and picture a tasbih in someone's hand. That is one form of it, but the word is much bigger. Dhikr means remembrance — keeping Allah in mind, on the tongue and in one's actions. The scholars describe it on three levels. Remembrance of the heart: thinking of Allah, being aware of Him, fearing and loving Him. Remembrance of the tongue: saying SubhanAllah (Glory be to Allah), Alhamdulillah (All praise belongs to Allah), Allahu Akbar (Allah is Greatest), La ilaha illallah (There is no god but Allah), and reciting the Quran. Remembrance of the limbs: living in obedience — prayer itself is dhikr, giving charity is dhikr, keeping a promise for Allah's sake is dhikr.

The Quran commands it without a limit. In the meaning of Quran 33:41-42, believers are told: "O you who believe, remember Allah with much remembrance, and glorify Him morning and evening." Notice the word "much". Most worship has a fixed measure — five prayers, a month of fasting — but dhikr is left open-ended, because the heart's need for its Lord has no ceiling. And in Quran 7:205, the manner is described: remember your Lord within yourself, with humility and awe, without raising your voice, morning and evening, and do not be among the heedless.

This is why dhikr is not only for the mosque or the prayer mat. Heedlessness is the disease of a heart that forgets its Creator for hours at a time, and dhikr is the cure taken in small doses all day long. Every time you pause and say Alhamdulillah for something good, or SubhanAllah at something beautiful, or Astaghfirullah after a mistake, you have pulled your heart back from heedlessness to remembrance. That pulling back, repeated a hundred times a day, is the whole practice.

2.'Remember Me, and I Will Remember You'

Of all the promises attached to dhikr, one stands above the rest for its sheer tenderness. In the meaning of Quran 2:152, Allah says: "So remember Me, and I will remember you." Think about the scale of that exchange. A servant, small and forgetful, turns his mind to his Lord — and the Lord of all the worlds turns to him in return. The verse continues with gratitude: be grateful to Me and do not deny Me. Remembrance and gratitude walk together, because a heart that remembers its blessings cannot help but thank the One who gave them.

A sacred narration (hadith qudsi), reported in Sahih al-Bukhari 7405 and Sahih Muslim 2675, opens this promise even wider. In its meaning, Allah says: "I am as My servant thinks of Me, and I am with him when he remembers Me." If he remembers Allah alone, Allah remembers him alone; if he remembers Allah in a gathering, Allah mentions him in a gathering better than it. The person who sits with a tasbih in a quiet room may feel alone. This narration tells him he is the opposite of alone: he is being mentioned, by name, in the highest company.

That is the first reason dhikr calms anxiety. Anxiety is, at its root, the feeling of facing life alone — alone with the bills, alone with the diagnosis, alone with the decision. Dhikr breaks the aloneness at its source. The heart that remembers is not managing life by itself; it is walking through it in the company of the One who controls it. 3.The Promise: Hearts Find Rest

The Quran's famous promise about dhikr comes in Surah Ar-Ra'd. In the meaning of Quran 13:28: "Those who believe, and whose hearts find rest in the remembrance of Allah — truly, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest." Read the order of that verse carefully. Faith comes first, then rest follows — not rest as a reward for a perfect life, but rest as the direct fruit of remembering. The hearts described here are not hearts without problems. They are hearts that have found, inside the problems, a place of stillness.

Why would remembering bring rest? Because worry is almost always a story about the future or the past — what might go wrong, what already went wrong — while dhikr pulls the heart into the present, before its Lord. A person saying SubhanAllah is declaring that Allah is free of all imperfection, including the imperfections of the plan he fears. A person saying Alhamdulillah is counting what is already given, not what is missing. A person saying Allahu Akbar is reminding himself that whatever is frightening him is smaller than Allah. Each phrase quietly shrinks the worry back to its true size.

The Quran adds, in the meaning of Quran 29:45, that the remembrance of Allah is greater — greater, the scholars explain, than every other deed in its effect on the heart, and greater than anything else in keeping a person from wrongdoing. And in Quran 48:4, Allah describes sending down tranquillity (sakinah) into the hearts of the believers so their faith may increase. Dhikr is one of the doors through which that tranquillity arrives. It does not remove the storm; it steadies the ship inside it.

4.The Living and the Dead

The Prophet (peace be upon him) drew the sharpest possible line between the person who remembers and the person who does not. In a narration reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim, he said, in meaning: "The example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not remember his Lord is like that of the living and the dead." Muslim's wording adds the picture of two houses: the house in which Allah is remembered and the house in which He is not are like the living and the dead.

That is a startling comparison, and it is meant to startle. A body can be walking, working and talking while its heart is dead — numb to its Creator, moved by nothing higher than its appetites and anxieties. Dhikr is what keeps the heart alive: sensitive, responsive, able to feel awe, gratitude and remorse. The person who remembers is awake to the reality behind everyday life. The person who never remembers can pass through an entire life half-asleep.

The reward of remembering together is even greater. In a narration reported in Sahih Muslim, the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught that no people gather to remember Allah except that the angels surround them, mercy covers them, tranquillity descends upon them, and Allah mentions them to those in His presence. A family that does dhikr together after prayer, a study circle that begins with remembrance, even two friends reminding each other of Allah — these are gatherings the angels attend. Truly, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest. — Quran 13:28, in meaning

5.Small Words, Enormous Reward

One of the mercies of dhikr is how little it costs for how much it returns. In Sahih al-Bukhari 6405, the Prophet (peace be upon him) said, in meaning, that whoever says SubhanAllah wa bihamdihi (Glory be to Allah and praise be to Him) one hundred times in a day will have his sins wiped away, even if they were like the foam of the sea. One hundred repetitions take about two minutes. The reward is described in an image no one can count: foam on the sea.

After each obligatory prayer, a beloved pattern is reported in Sahih Muslim 597: say SubhanAllah thirty-three times, Alhamdulillah thirty-three times, and Allahu Akbar thirty-three times. Five prayers a day, under two minutes each time, and the day is stitched together with remembrance at its five fixed points. This is the genius of the Sunnah's dhikr: it does not ask for a separate hour carved out of an impossible schedule. It attaches itself to moments that already exist — after prayer, morning and evening, before sleep — and fills the gaps between them.

None of this requires perfection of pronunciation on the first day, or a tasbih, or a quiet room. The Prophet's companions did dhikr on journeys, in markets and at work. What matters is presence of heart as far as one is able — even a distracted dhikr, kept up, is better than a perfect dhikr abandoned. Allah rewards the effort, not only the feeling. The feeling, with time and persistence, usually follows the effort.

6.Five Ways to Fill Your Day with Dhikr

Dhikr grows the way any habit grows: by being attached to something you already do. These five ways are deliberately small. Pick one this week, and let it become automatic before adding another.

1. Anchor it to the five prayers. After every prayer, before you reach for your phone, say the thirty-three times pattern from Sahih Muslim 597. It takes under two minutes and turns the end of prayer into a bridge, not a cliff edge. The day stops being five isolated prayers and becomes one continuous remembrance with five peaks.

2. Bookend the day. Morning and evening are the two times the Quran names for remembrance (Quran 33:41-42, 7:205). Keep it simple: a few minutes after Fajr and a few minutes before Maghrib, saying SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu Akbar and Astaghfirullah with attention. Our earlier article on blessed mornings and the deeds after Fajr gives a fuller morning routine to build on.

3. Use the in-between minutes. Commutes, queues, cooking, waiting rooms — these are the hours most people hand to scrolling. Carry a small tasbih, or simply count on your fingers, and fill one such gap a day with La ilaha illallah or SubhanAllah wa bihamdihi. You will be surprised how quickly dead time becomes the most peaceful part of the day.

4. Let the Quran be your dhikr. Reciting the Quran is itself among the highest forms of remembrance, because it is Allah's own words. Even a few verses a day, read slowly with their meaning, counts as dhikr of the tongue and the heart together. If you cannot read Arabic fluently, reading a reliable translation with reflection is still time spent with Allah's words, not away from them.

5. Remember in hardship first, not last. Most people turn to dhikr only when everything else has failed. Reverse the order: when bad news arrives, before calling anyone, say Inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un (To Allah we belong, and to Him we return) and Hasbunallahu wa ni'mal wakeel (Allah is sufficient for us, and He is the best Disposer of affairs). Remembering first does not mean not taking action; it means taking action with a heart that is already at rest.
