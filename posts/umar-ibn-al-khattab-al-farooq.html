Umar ibn al-Khattab (RA): The Justice of Al-Farooq

R Razia Azeem · 25 Nov, 2026

Seerah & Stories

He set out one morning with his sword at his side and murder in his heart. By sunset, the man who had wanted to kill the Prophet ﷺ was a Muslim — and the Muslims of Makkah walked to the Kaaba in two rows, openly, for the first time. That is the story of Umar ibn al-Khattab (may Allah be pleased with him): the fiercest opponent of Islam became its fiercest defender, and the second caliph became the standard by which justice is still measured.

1.The Man Who Set Out with a Sword

Umar ibn al-Khattab, of Banu Adi, was tall, strong, and feared in Makkah — one of the few Quraysh who could read and write, a wrestler and a horseman whose word carried weight. When Islam began to spread, he opposed it with all his strength. The seerah tells us that one day, in his anger at the new faith dividing his people, he took up his sword and set out to kill the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ himself.

On the way, a man told him that his own sister Fatimah bint al-Khattab and her husband Sa'id ibn Zayd had already accepted Islam. Umar turned aside to their house in fury — and found them reading from a sheet of the Quran. He struck his sister until she bled, and then, seeing the blood on her face, his anger broke. He asked to see what they had been reading. It was the opening of Surah Ta-Ha. He read.

He went at once to the house of Al-Arqam, where the Prophet ﷺ was staying with his companions. Hamzah (may Allah be pleased with him), who was there, said — as the seerah reports — "Let him in. If he comes with good intent, we will welcome him; if not, we will deal with him with his own sword." Umar entered, and the Prophet ﷺ took him by his garment and asked him what had brought him. Umar answered: "I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and that you are the Messenger of Allah." The seerah reports that after that day the Muslims came out in two rows — Hamzah leading one, Umar the other — and prayed openly at the Kaaba for the first time.

2.A Dua Answered Before He Arrived

What Umar did not know was that the Prophet ﷺ had already prayed for him — Ibn Umar narrated that he said: "O Allah! Honor Islam through the most dear of these two men to you: Through Abu Jahl or through 'Umar bin Al-Khattab." And the narrator added: "And the most dear of them to Him was 'Umar" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3681, graded Hasan).

Think about that. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for the man who was, at that very moment, his enemy — a man walking the streets with a sword meant for him. He did not pray for Umar's destruction; he prayed for his guidance. And Allah answered by changing the man's heart.

The person who seems furthest from guidance today may be one dua away from it.

3."If There Were a Prophet After Me"

'Uqbah ibn 'Amir narrated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "If there was to have a Prophet after me, it would have been 'Umar bin Al-Khattab" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3686, graded Hasan). It was a measure of his rank: the closest a man could come to prophethood without being a prophet.

Ibn Umar narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Indeed Allah has put the truth upon the tongue and in the heart of 'Umar" — and added that whenever a matter arose and the people said one thing and Umar said another, the Quran was revealed in line with what Umar had said (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3682, graded Hasan Sahih Gharib). And Anas ibn Malik narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "The most merciful of my nation to my nation is Abu Bakr, and the most severe of them concerning the order of Allah is 'Umar" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3790, graded Hasan Gharib). Abu Bakr was mercy; Umar was firmness — and the Ummah needed both.

Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas narrated the scene that shows what that firmness looked like. Umar asked permission to enter while some Qurayshi women were sitting with the Prophet ﷺ, raising their voices and asking for more expenses. As soon as they heard Umar's voice, they quickly put on their veils. The Prophet ﷺ smiled and said: "O Ibn Al-Khattab! By Him in Whose Hands my life is! Never does Satan find you going on a way, but he takes another way other than yours" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3683). Even Satan changed his route when Umar came down the road.

4."My Lord Agreed with Me in Three Things"

Anas ibn Malik narrated that Umar said: "I agreed with Allah in three things," or, "My Lord agreed with me in three things" — and named them: the Station of Abraham as a place of prayer, the veiling of the Mothers of the Believers, and the warning to the Prophet's wives (Sahih al-Bukhari 4483).

The first was the Station of Ibrahim — the stone on which Ibrahim (peace be upon him) stood while building the Kaaba. Umar suggested praying behind it, and Allah revealed:

وَٱتَّخِذُوا۟ مِن مَّقَامِ إِبْرَٰهِۦمَ مُصَلًّۭى

Quran 2:125 — "And take the Station of Abraham as a place of prayer."

The second was the veil. Umar suggested that the Mothers of the Believers cover themselves, and Allah revealed the verse of hijab:

وَإِذَا سَأَلْتُمُوهُنَّ مَتَٰعًۭا فَسْـَٔلُوهُنَّ مِن وَرَآءِ حِجَابٍۢ

Quran 33:53 — "And when you ask [the Prophet's wives] for something, ask them from behind a partition."

The third was about the Prophet's wives: Umar warned them that if they kept troubling the Prophet ﷺ, Allah would give him better wives instead — and Allah revealed: "It may be, if he divorced you (all), his Lord will give him instead of you, wives better than you, Muslims (who submit to Allah)" (Quran 66:5, as quoted in Sahih al-Bukhari 4483). Three times, Umar spoke a view — and revelation came down in agreement with it. That is why the Muslims called him Al-Farooq: the Criterion, the one who distinguishes truth from falsehood.

5.The Caliph Who Walked the Streets at Night

When Abu Bakr (may Allah be pleased with him) died in 13 AH, he named Umar as his successor. Umar ruled ten years — 13 to 23 AH (634–644 CE) — and in that decade the armies under his command took the Levant, Persia and Egypt. But the histories remember him less for the conquests than for what he did at night.

Umar walked the streets of Madinah after dark, alone, checking on his people. The histories tell of a night when he found a widow with hungry children crying over an empty pot, and carried a sack of flour on his own back to her tent — refusing his servant's offer, saying (as the histories report) he would not let another man carry his burden on the Day of Judgement. A saying attributed to him captures the fear that drove him: "If a mule stumbled on the road to Iraq, I fear Allah will ask me why I did not level the road." The caliph of half the known world feared being asked about a mule.

He built the state as carefully as he watched it: he established the diwan — the register of stipends — and in 17 AH the Hijri calendar, counting the years from the Hijrah itself. And he gathered the people of Madinah behind one reciter for the night prayer of Ramadan: 'Abdur-Rahman ibn 'Abdul-Qari said he went out with Umar one night in Ramadan and found the people praying in scattered groups, so Umar said he would gather them under one reciter, and he gathered them behind Ubayy ibn Ka'b — and remarked, "What an excellent Bid'a (innovation in religion) this is" (Sahih al-Bukhari 2010). The tarawih congregation we pray in today began with Umar's decision that night.

6.Justice That Began at Home

Umar's justice began with himself and his own family. His famous letter to his judge Abu Musa al-Ash'ari instructed him to treat people equally in his court, in his gaze and in his judgement — rich and poor alike. That was the rule he lived by. The histories report that when his son Abdullah's camels grew fat grazing on the public pasture, Umar ordered them sold and the profit paid into the treasury — the caliph's own son got no advantage from his father's name.

When Jerusalem surrendered, Umar travelled to accept it himself — and when invited to pray inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, the histories report that he refused, praying outside instead, so that no Muslim after him would claim the church as a mosque. Justice, for Umar, meant restraint even in victory.

The Quran's command is the one he lived by:

كُونُوا۟ قَوَّٰمِينَ بِٱلْقِسْطِ شُهَدَآءَ لِلَّهِ وَلَوْ عَلَىٰٓ أَنفُسِكُمْ

Quran 4:135 — "O you who believe! Stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to Allah, even if it be against yourselves, your parents, and your relatives."

"Even if it be against yourselves" — that is the hardest part of the verse, and the part Umar embodied. For how justice stands even against yourself, read Justice (Adl): Standing Firm for Truth, Even Against Yourself. And for the man who came before him, read Abu Bakr as-Siddiq (RA): The Closest Companion of the Prophet ﷺ.

7.The Martyr of Fajr — and What He Leaves Us

Umar was martyred as he lived: in prayer. A Persian captive named Abu Lu'lu'ah, a slave of Al-Mughirah ibn Shu'bah, stabbed him during the Fajr prayer on 26 Dhul-Hijjah 23 AH (644 CE). He died three days later and was buried beside the Prophet ﷺ and Abu Bakr — the three friends together, as in life. Before he died, he appointed a council of six to choose the next caliph, refusing to name his own son: the caliphate was a trust, not an inheritance.

On his deathbed, as the histories report, he reminded those around him that there is no share in Islam for the one who abandons prayer. The man who had ruled half the world died worrying about the prayer.

1. Pray for your enemies by name. The Prophet ﷺ prayed for Umar while Umar was his enemy — and Allah answered before Umar ever arrived.

2. Let your word match the truth. The Prophet ﷺ said Allah put truth on Umar's tongue and in his heart (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3682). Say what is true even when it costs you — especially then. That is what made Umar Al-Farooq.

3. Justice begins at home. Stand for what is right even when it is against your own interests — "even if it be against yourselves" (Quran 4:135). For the battle where the Prophet's ﷺ companions learned obedience the hard way, read The Battle of Uhud: Lessons from the Mountain.

4. Fear the small account. Ask yourself tonight: what small trust — a promise, a debt, a duty — am I carrying that I have not settled? Settle it before you are asked about it.

"If there was to have a Prophet after me, it would have been 'Umar bin Al-Khattab." — the Prophet ﷺ (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3686, Hasan)

Umar began as the man with the sword and ended as the man with the prayer. Between those two moments lies the whole of Islam: a heart changed by the Quran, a life spent in justice, and a death in the prayer he loved. May Allah be pleased with him — and give us a share of his courage, his justice, and his fear of the account.
