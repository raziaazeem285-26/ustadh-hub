Silat ar-Rahim: Why Islam Commands Keeping Ties of Kinship

R Razia Azeem · 23 Oct, 2026

Two brothers who have not spoken in three years over an old inheritance dispute. A cousin whose calls stopped being returned. A family WhatsApp group gone silent. In many homes, the ties of kinship are quietly breaking — and Islam treats that as a very serious matter. Here is what the Quran and Hadith say about keeping ties with family, and five small steps to rebuild a broken bond today.

Daily Life

Almost every family has one — a rift no one remembers starting, a relative no one calls any more. We tell ourselves that staying silent is neutral. Islam says otherwise. Keeping the ties of kinship, silat ar-rahim, is not a nice extra for good-hearted people. It is a command of Allah, tied to belief in Him and the Last Day, and it carries some of the most beautiful promises in all of our religion.

1.What Is Silat ar-Rahim?

Silat ar-rahim means joining and maintaining the bonds of kinship — parents, siblings, uncles and aunts, cousins, and all those Allah has linked to us by blood and marriage. The word rahm means "womb": our relatives are literally those with whom we share one origin, one womb of the family.

It is worth knowing that the word rahm is connected to one of Allah's own names, Ar-Rahman (the Most Merciful). Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said the word ar-rahm (womb) derives its name from Ar-Rahman (Sahih al-Bukhari). The tie itself is woven from mercy — and mercy is what the whole command is about.

2.Kinship in the Quran: A Command, Not a Suggestion

The Quran does not treat kinship as optional advice. It places it beside the most sacred things. In Surah An-Nisa, Allah says: "And fear Allah, through whom you ask one another, and (be mindful of) the ties of kinship" (Quran 4:1). Kinship is mentioned in the very same breath as the fear of Allah.

Elsewhere, Allah describes the people of understanding — ulu al-albab — as "those who join that which Allah has ordered to be joined, and fear their Lord" (Quran 13:21). Joining the family bond is placed beside fearing Allah and praying and giving charity.

And the warning for cutting those ties is severe. Allah says of those who spread corruption on earth and sever their ties of kinship: "It is those whom Allah has cursed, so He deafened them and blinded their sight" (Quran 47:22–23). No one reading these verses can treat a family feud as a small thing.

3.The Promise: Wider Provision, Longer Life

Kinship is not only duty — it is also a doorway of blessing. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever wishes that his provision be extended for him and his lifespan prolonged, let him maintain the ties of kinship" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5984). Keeping family ties is one of the rare deeds that the Prophet ﷺ directly linked to more rizq and a longer life.

It is also a test of faith itself. Abu Hurairah (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him maintain the ties of kinship" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5985; Sahih Muslim 2556). The same hadith tells the believer to honor the guest and to speak good or stay silent — and right between them stands the command of kinship.

Even charity changes its value when given to family. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Charity to the poor is one charity; charity to a relative is two — charity and maintaining the tie" (Sunan Abi Dawud 1693, graded sahih by al-Albani). That Eid gift or grocery bag for a struggling cousin is doubled in the scales.

4.The Womb Clinging to Allah's Throne

Perhaps the most striking image in all of the hadith about family is this one. Aisha (may Allah be pleased with her) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said:

"The womb (ar-rahm) is attached to the Throne, saying: 'Whoever joins me, Allah joins him; and whoever cuts me, Allah cuts him.'" — Sahih al-Bukhari 5987; Sahih Muslim 2555

الرَّحِمُ مُعَلَّقَةٌ بِالْعَرْشِ تَقُولُ: مَنْ وَصَلَنِي وَصَلَهُ اللَّهُ، وَمَنْ قَطَعَنِي قَطَعَهُ اللَّهُ

Think about what this means. The tie of kinship itself stands before Allah and speaks. It pleads for those who keep it and warns against those who break it. Every phone call to an aging mother, every visit to a cousin, every reconciling message to a brother is registered in that court. And every deliberate cutting of that tie is, too.

5.The True Test: Joining When You Are Cut Off

Here is where most of us fail — and where Islam asks the most. A man once came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "O Messenger of Allah, I have relatives with whom I maintain ties but they cut me off. I do good to them, but they treat me badly." Listen to what the Prophet ﷺ told him: "If it is as you say, it is as if you are feeding them hot ashes — and Allah will keep supporting you against them as long as you persist in that" (Sahih Muslim 2558).

The Prophet ﷺ did not tell him to cut them back. He told him to persist — and promised that Allah Himself would support the one who keeps joining.

And he defined, with stunning clarity, who truly keeps the ties. Abdullah ibn Amr (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Prophet ﷺ said:

لَيْسَ الْوَاصِلُ بِالْمُكَافِئِ، وَلَكِنَّ الْوَاصِلَ الَّذِي إِذَا انْقَطَعَتْ رَحِمُهُ وَصَلَهَا

"The one who maintains ties is not the one who merely reciprocates. The true keeper of ties is the one who joins them when they are cut off" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1908, graded sahih by al-Albani). Answering kindness with kindness is easy. Calling the uncle who never calls you — that is silat ar-rahim.

If you struggle with anger when dealing with difficult relatives, you may also find help in our recent article on controlling anger in Islam — sabr and pardoning go hand in hand with keeping family ties.

6.Five Small Ways to Rebuild a Tie Today

You do not need a grand gesture. Kinship is rebuilt with small, consistent steps:

1. Send a salaam first. A short message — "Assalamu alaikum, just thinking of you, how are the children?" — costs nothing and breaks years of ice. Do not wait for them to move first.

2. Call instead of texting, when you can. A voice carries warmth a text never will. A weekly call to a parent, an uncle, or an aunt keeps the tie alive.

3. Share food or a small gift. The Prophet ﷺ taught that gifts grow love between people. Cook a little extra and send a plate; on Eid, a gift for a cousin you rarely see says what words cannot.

4. Make dua for them — by name. The tie of kinship includes the tie of prayer. Asking Allah to bless, guide, and ease the affairs of a difficult relative softens your own heart toward them.

5. Keep showing up, quietly. Visit on Eid. Attend the family wedding. Greet the relative who gossips about you. Consistency over months does what one dramatic apology often cannot.

And remember: keeping ties does not mean accepting harm. A short, respectful greeting still counts as joining; you are not commanded to endure abuse. The command is to keep the door open — not to walk into fire.
