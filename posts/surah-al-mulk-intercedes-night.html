Surah Al-Mulk: The Surah That Intercedes for You at Night

R Razia Azeem · 4 Nov, 2026

Thirty short verses, recited each night — yet the Prophet (peace be upon him) said this surah will keep pleading for its companion until he is forgiven. That is the promise attached to Surah Al-Mulk, and it is why so many Muslims end their day with it.

Quran

There are surahs we recite because they are long and grand, and surahs we recite because they are short and familiar. Surah Al-Mulk belongs to a rarer group: a surah Muslims return to every single night, not because it is easy — though at thirty verses it is short — but because of what the Prophet (peace be upon him) said it does for the person who keeps it close. It speaks for you. It pleads for you. It stands between you and punishment. No wonder the early Muslims called it by a special name: al-Mani'ah, the Protector.

This article looks at what Surah Al-Mulk actually says, the authentic narrations about its virtue, and the simple nightly habit that turns those thirty verses from something you have heard about into something that belongs to your day. You do not need to be a scholar to begin. You only need a few quiet minutes before sleep.

1.A Surah of Only Thirty Verses

Surah Al-Mulk is the 67th chapter of the Quran, revealed in Makkah, and it contains just thirty verses. It takes most readers about five minutes to recite. That brevity is part of its gift: unlike longer chapters that demand a sitting, Al-Mulk fits into the small, tired space at the end of the day, between Isha prayer and sleep. A child can memorise it in a few weeks, a busy adult can learn it verse by verse, and anyone who already knows it can recite it without a mushaf, in bed, in prayer, or on a quiet walk.

The surah is also called Tabarak, after its opening word: "Blessed is He in whose hand is dominion" (Quran 67:1). Its name tells you its subject before you read a single verse. This is a surah about who is really in charge. Not kings, not wealth, not the powers people spend their lives impressing — but Allah, in whose hand all dominion rests, and who has power over all things. Every verse that follows is an argument for that opening claim, drawn from the sky above you, the earth beneath you, and the life you are living right now.

2.Blessed Is He in Whose Hand Is Dominion

The surah opens with a statement of total sovereignty, in meaning: Blessed is He in whose hand is dominion, and He is over all things competent (Quran 67:1). The word "tabarak" carries the sense of overflowing blessing and exaltedness — Allah's goodness is not small or scarce. And the dominion is in His hand, not shared, not borrowed, not at risk. For a believer lying awake worrying about money, health, children, or the future, this is the first medicine of the surah: whatever you fear losing was never ultimately in anyone else's hand.

Then the surah invites you to look up. In meaning: He who created seven heavens in layers. You do not see in the creation of the Most Merciful any inconsistency. So return your vision to the sky; do you see any breaks? Then return your vision twice again; your vision will return to you humbled while it is fatigued (Quran 67:3-4). The argument is gentle but devastating to arrogance: look for a flaw in the heavens and you will only tire your own eyes. The universe is not random and it is not broken; it is a creation held together by a Creator. If the One who built seven heavens without a crack is the One in whose hand your life rests, then your life is held by capable hands.

3.The Test of Death and Life

The second verse gives the surah its purpose, in meaning: He who created death and life to test you as to which of you is best in deed — and He is the Exalted in Might, the Forgiving (Quran 67:2). Notice the order: death is mentioned before life. We usually think of life as the main event and death as the ending, but the Quran reverses it here to correct our perspective. Death is not an interruption of the test; it is part of the test's design. And the test is not about who does the most deeds, but who does the best deeds — quality over quantity, sincerity over show.

The same verse ends with two names of Allah placed side by side: the Exalted in Might, the Forgiving. Power and forgiveness together — that pairing runs through the whole surah. Later verses remind us, in meaning, that those who fear their Lord unseen will have forgiveness and great reward (Quran 67:12), and that the birds held above us in the sky are upheld by none but the Most Merciful (Quran 67:19). A bird does not store up wealth or plan its retirement, yet it is fed; a believer who fears Allah unseen is forgiven, yet still strives. The surah holds both truths at once: Allah is mighty enough to judge, and merciful enough to forgive — and the wise heart lives between fear of Him and hope in Him.

4.The Intercession: A Companion That Speaks for You

Now we come to the narration that made this surah famous. Abu Hurayrah (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Prophet (peace be upon him) said, in meaning: there is a surah of the Quran, thirty verses, which interceded for a man until he was forgiven — and it is Surah Tabarak, "Blessed is He in whose hand is dominion" (Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2891, graded hasan). Picture that scene: on the Day when every person stands alone, a surah you used to recite in the dark of your bedroom steps forward and speaks for you, and keeps speaking until you are forgiven.

"There is a surah of the Quran, thirty verses, which interceded for a man until he was forgiven — and it is Surah Tabarak." — Jami' at-Tirmidhi 2891, in meaning

Two cautions keep this hope honest. First, no intercession happens except by Allah's permission — the Quran is explicit, in meaning: who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission? (Quran 2:255). The surah's intercession is a gift Allah grants, not a right we can demand. Second, scholars explain that the intercession belongs to the surah's companion — the person who recites it regularly, believes what it says, and lives by it — not to someone who merely owns a copy. Recitation, belief, and practice travel together. The surah pleads for the person who kept its company in life; that is why it is worth making it a nightly companion now.

5.The Nightly Habit of the Prophet ﷺ

The Prophet (peace be upon him) did not merely recommend this surah; he lived with it. Jabir ibn Abdullah (may Allah be pleased with him) reported that the Messenger of Allah would not go to sleep until he had recited Surah As-Sajdah and Surah Al-Mulk (Jami' at-Tirmidhi, graded authentic by scholars). That is the model: two surahs, every night, before sleep — a habit so regular that his companions could describe his bedtime by it.

The companions took the same habit for themselves. Abdullah ibn Mas'ud (may Allah be pleased with him) said, in meaning: whoever recites "Tabarak, in whose hand is dominion" every night, Allah will protect him by it from the punishment of the grave — and he added that in the time of the Messenger they used to call it al-Mani'ah, the Protector (reported in Sunan an-Nasa'i, graded hasan by scholars). That is why the surah is so closely tied to the night: it is the last thing you recite before the small death of sleep, a reminder of the greater reality it describes, and — by Allah's mercy — a protection in the grave that follows this life. Scholars note that the virtue described is attached to reciting the surah, so reading it yourself, even slowly, is the habit to build; listening alone, beautiful as it is, is not the same as recitation.

6.How to Make Al-Mulk Part of Your Night

Starting tonight is simpler than you think. First, fix a moment: right after Isha, or as you get into bed, before the phone takes over. Five minutes is enough. If you cannot yet read Arabic fluently, read along with a transliteration or listen and repeat line by line — the goal is your own recitation, at your own pace, and fluency comes with repetition. Second, learn it in pieces: the surah divides naturally into short passages, and memorising two or three verses a week means the whole surah is yours within a few months. Third, recite it in prayer sometimes — even one passage in a night prayer makes the surah part of your worship rather than only your bedtime routine.

Finally, teach it to someone. A child who learns Al-Mulk from you will recite it for the rest of their life, and every recitation carries reward back to the one who taught it. That is how a thirty-verse surah becomes a lifetime companion: one night at a time, one verse at a time, until the night feels incomplete without it.
