Kindness to Animals: A Forgotten Part of Ihsan

R Razia Azeem · 17 Nov, 2026

A shoe full of water for a thirsty dog erased a lifetime of sins — while cruelty to a cat led to the Fire. What the Quran and Hadith really teach about kindness to animals, and how to live it on an ordinary day.

Daily Life

A man walked down a road, dying of thirst. He climbed down a well, drank, and came back up — only to find a dog panting at the edge, licking the mud in its desperation. The man thought: this dog is suffering exactly as I suffered. So he climbed down again, filled his shoe with water, carried it up in his teeth, and gave the dog to drink. For that one small act, Allah thanked him — and forgave his sins.

That is not a story about a great battle or a famous victory. It is a story about a shoe full of water. And it sits at the very heart of what Islam teaches about animals: that mercy is not reserved for human beings, that kindness to a creature who cannot speak for itself is a deed Allah notices, and that cruelty to one is a sin Allah does not overlook. This is a forgotten part of ihsan — excellence in every single thing, even in how we treat a stray dog on a hot afternoon.

1.Communities Like You

The Quran begins by changing how we see animals. They are not background props in the human story, and they are not machines without feeling. Allah says:

وَمَا مِن دَابَّةٍ فِي الْأَرْضِ وَلَا طَائِرٍ يَطِيرُ بِجَنَاحَيْهِ إِلَّا أُمَمٌ أَمْثَالُكُمْ

Quran 6:38

"And there is no creature on earth, nor a bird that flies with its wings, but they are communities like you." Every species is an ummah — a community with its own life, its own way of living, its own place in Allah's creation. The same Quran tells us that Allah created every living creature from water (Quran 24:45). They did not appear by accident, and they were not made only to serve us.

That is why the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was not sent as a mercy only to human beings. Allah says: "And We have not sent you, O Muhammad, except as a mercy to the worlds" (Quran 21:107). The worlds — all of them. The mercy he brought covers the human, the animal, the bird, and even the ant. When we remember that, kindness to animals stops looking like a small, optional extra. It becomes part of the mercy itself.

2.A Drink of Water That Erased a Lifetime of Sins

The story of the thirsty dog is reported in Sahih al-Bukhari (2363), and its ending is what makes it unforgettable. When the Companions heard it, they asked the obvious question: "O Messenger of Allah, is there a reward for us in serving animals?" The Prophet ﷺ replied: "There is a reward for showing kindness to every living thing."

"There is a reward for showing kindness to every living thing." — Sahih al-Bukhari 2363

Every living thing. Not every human being — every living thing. The reward is not measured by the size of the deed or the status of the creature. A shoe of water counts. And the same collection reports an even more striking story: a woman from Bani Israel, whose life was full of sin, saw a dog circling a well, almost dying of thirst. She took off her shoe, drew water with it, and gave the dog to drink — and Allah forgave her for it (Sahih al-Bukhari). One act of mercy towards a creature that could never repay her, never thank her, never even remember her — and it became the deed that changed her ending.

Think about what that means for us. We often wait for a grand opportunity to earn reward: a big donation, a major project, a public act of charity. But Allah's mercy is so wide that a bowl of water on a summer afternoon can carry the weight of a lifetime.

3.The Other Side of the Same Scale

If a small kindness can erase sins, a small cruelty can earn punishment — and the Prophet ﷺ warned about this with equal clarity. Sahih al-Bukhari (2365) reports that a woman was punished because of a cat she had locked up until it died. She neither fed it nor gave it water, nor did she set it free to find food for itself. She entered the Fire because of that cat.

Read that again slowly. Not because of murder, not because of theft, not because of a great crime — because of a cat, locked in a room, left hungry. The message is not that the cat mattered more than people. The message is that cruelty is cruelty, no matter how small the victim, and Allah sees it. A narration in Sunan an-Nasa'i carries the same warning further: whoever kills even a small bird without a just cause will be held accountable for it by Allah.

The Prophet ﷺ also forbade inciting animals to fight each other for sport (Sahih al-Bukhari). Making creatures suffer for entertainment was never a game in his eyes — it was a sin. That ruling reaches into our own time: dog fights, cockfights, videos of animals tormented for laughs. If the creature's pain is the entertainment, the entertainment is forbidden.

One honest note belongs here. You may have heard the beloved story of a cat sleeping on the Prophet's robe, and the Prophet cutting the cloth rather than waking it. It is a popular story, told with love in many homes — but it is not established in the authentic hadith collections, so we leave it aside. We do not need it. What is authentically established about mercy to animals is already more than enough.

4.The Camel That Complained, and the Bird That Grieved

Animals in the Prophet's ﷺ life were not silent objects. They had feelings he recognised, and he taught his Companions to recognise them too. Once, he entered a garden in Madinah and a camel came to him, weeping, its eyes flowing with tears. The Prophet ﷺ asked who its owner was, and when the young man came forward, he said: "Fear Allah in the way you deal with this animal of which Allah has granted you ownership. It complained to me that you overwork it and make it go hungry" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2549).

The camel complained — and the Prophet listened. An owner can be busy, can be tired, can have a hundred excuses, but the animal still gets hungry, still gets exhausted, and its hunger is the owner's responsibility. That is the meaning of ownership in Islam: not a right to use, but a trust to keep.

On a journey, the Companions found a mother bird with two chicks and took the young ones. The mother bird came back, found her babies gone, and began beating her wings in distress. When the Prophet ﷺ returned he asked: "Who has grieved this bird by taking her young? Return them to her." In the same incident he saw an ant village the Companions had burned, and said: "It is not proper to punish with fire except the Lord of fire" (Sunan Abi Dawud 2675). The bird's grief mattered. The ants' lives mattered. Even the smallest creatures are covered by the same mercy.

5.Ihsan — Even at the Moment of Slaughter

Islam does not pretend that humans never use animals. We eat meat, we ride, we work with them. But even where use is lawful, cruelty is not. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Verily, Allah has prescribed ihsan in all things. So if you kill, kill well; and if you slaughter, slaughter well. Let one of you sharpen his blade and spare suffering to the animal he slaughters" (Sahih Muslim 1955).

Ihsan — excellence, beauty, doing a thing in the best possible way — is prescribed in all things. All things. Not just prayer and charity, but the knife, the slaughter, the handling of a living creature in its last moments. The scholars who explain this hadith draw out the practical details: sharpen the blade before, not in front of the animal; do not drag it harshly; do not slaughter one animal where another can watch. The animal's fear counts. Its pain counts. Even its last sight counts.

This is the measure of a faith: not how we treat creatures when it is easy and costless, but how we treat them when nobody is watching and there is something to gain by being rough. A dull knife is cheaper to keep. A hurried slaughter is faster. Ihsan says: do it the harder, kinder way anyway, because Allah prescribed excellence — and He is watching.

6.What Kindness Looks Like on an Ordinary Day

So what does all of this mean on an ordinary Tuesday, for a family in a city flat or a village house? It means small, concrete habits — the kind that cost almost nothing and count for everything.

First, water and food. In the summer heat, a bowl of water outside the door can save a stray dog, a cat, a bird. The Prophet ﷺ said that if a Muslim plants a tree or sows a seed, and a bird, a person, or an animal eats from it, it is counted as charity for him (Sahih al-Bukhari). The tree you plant may feed creatures you will never meet, and the reward keeps coming.

Second, responsibility for the animals in our care. A pet is not a toy; it is an amanah, a trust. Food, clean water, shelter from heat and cold, treatment when it is sick — these are not kindnesses we choose to offer, they are duties we owe. If we cannot care for an animal properly, we should not take it on, and we must never simply abandon one that depends on us.

Third, gentleness in daily contact. No hitting out of anger, no overloading a working animal, no tying an animal in the sun, no keeping a caged bird hungry. Children learn mercy by watching us: the way a father speaks to a stray dog teaches his child more about Islam than a hundred lectures. Teach children to feed the birds, to move a worm off the path, to never throw stones at a cat — and you have taught them mercy, which is the heart of the faith itself.

Fourth, respect in what we consume, and refuse cruelty dressed as fun: no animal fights, no videos that torment creatures for clicks, no "games" where an animal's fear is the joke. Gentleness is not weakness — as gentleness softens hearts, it is the strength of a believer.

7.A Mercy That Starts Small

Here is the beautiful symmetry of this teaching. A drink of water erased a lifetime of sins. A locked door and an unfed cat earned the Fire. The scale of Allah is not the scale of the world: the world measures deeds by their size and their audience, while Allah measures them by the heart behind them and the creature in front of them.

Start this week with one small thing. Put a bowl of water outside your door and refill it every morning. Feed one stray. Plant one tree, even a small one, with the intention that birds and people will eat from it. Check on a pet you own — really check: food, water, shelter, health.

The Prophet ﷺ was a mercy to the worlds — and the worlds include the dog at the well, the cat at the door, the camel in the garden, and the bird over her nest. If we want a share of that mercy, the road is open, and it starts with a shoe full of water. May Allah fill our hearts with the mercy He placed in His Prophet, and may He count our smallest kindnesses among our greatest deeds. Ameen.
