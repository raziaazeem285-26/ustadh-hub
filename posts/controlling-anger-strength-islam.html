Controlling Anger: The Strength Islam Calls True Power

R Razia Azeem · 31 Oct, 2026

True strength is not winning an argument — it is controlling yourself when anger rises. What the Quran and Hadith teach about anger, and seven practical steps to master it.

In this article
  1. Anger is a feeling, not a verdict
  2. What the Quran says about restraining anger
  3. What the Prophet ﷺ taught about anger
  4. Why anger does so much damage
  5. Seven practical steps when anger rises
  6. When anger is right — and how to repair after it

Daily Life

Almost everyone knows the feeling: a harsh word, an unfair decision, a child who will not listen — and suddenly the voice is raised, and words are out before thought has caught up. Later comes the regret. The relationship is bruised, the apology feels small next to what was said, and you wonder why a moment of anger cost so much.

Islam does not pretend anger does not exist, nor tell you to become a person without feelings. It does something far more useful: it redefines strength. To the world, strength is winning the argument and having the last word. In Islam, strength is the opposite — holding yourself when you have every reason, and every opportunity, to explode. This article looks at what the Quran and Hadith actually teach about anger, why it causes so much damage, and seven practical steps, drawn from the Sunnah, for mastering it.

1.Anger is a feeling, not a verdict

The first thing to understand is that feeling angry is not itself a sin. Anger is a human emotion, placed in us for a reason. It alerts us when something is wrong, when a boundary has been crossed, when injustice has been done. The problem is never the flash of feeling; it is what we do in the next ten seconds. Do we speak, or stay silent? Do we strike back, or step back? Do we let the feeling pass through us, or hand it the steering wheel?

Many people fight the wrong battle: they feel guilty for being angry at all, and then explode anyway. Islam separates the two cleanly. The Quran praises not people who never feel anger — such people do not exist — but people who, when they are angered, forgive (Quran 42:37). The test is not whether anger visits you, it always will, but whether it rules you.

There is also an important exception worth stating plainly. The Prophet ﷺ himself became visibly angry when the limits of Allah were crossed — but never for a personal slight, a wounded ego, or a blow to his own pride. That is the line: anger for the sake of Allah, expressed with justice and dignity, is one thing; anger that serves only our ego, expressed by humiliating someone else, is quite another. Most of the anger that ruins our days belongs firmly in the second category.

2.What the Quran says about restraining anger

The Quran describes the people of Paradise-bound character in a verse that every Muslim should know by heart. Describing the righteous, Allah says they are people “who spend in the cause of Allah during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people — and Allah loves the doers of good” (Quran 3:134). Notice the order: restraining anger first, pardoning people second. Holding back is the first victory; forgiving is the higher one. And the verse ends with a promise: Allah loves the doers of good. Self-control is not repression for its own sake — it is an act of goodness that Allah loves.

A second verse, in Surah Ash-Shura, lists the same quality among the marks of true believers: “when they are angered, they forgive” (Quran 42:37). Forgiveness here is not weakness or approval of wrongdoing. It is a decision: I will not let your wrong define my response. The person who forgives has refused to let the argument own him.

Then the Quran gives us the higher road, the response that actually changes people. “Repel evil with that which is better” (Quran 41:34). A gentle answer to a harsh word, patience in the face of provocation, a kind word where a cutting one was expected — the Quran says this can turn an enemy into a close friend. It is not easy, but it is the only response that reliably ends a cycle of retaliation instead of continuing it.

Finally, look at the Prophetﷺ himself, as the Quran describes him: “So by mercy from Allah, you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude in speech and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you” (Quran 3:159). Gentleness was not a personality quirk of the Prophet ﷺ. It was the reason people stayed, listened, and changed. Harshness drives people away — from us, and sometimes, tragically, from the message we were trying to defend.

3.What the Prophet ﷺ taught about anger

The most famous hadith on this subject turns our whole idea of strength upside down. Abu Hurairah (may Allah be pleased with him) narrated that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

“The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength, but the strong is the one who controls himself while in anger.” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6114)

The wrestler who pins his opponent is not the strong one. The strong one is the person standing in the middle of an argument, heart pounding, with a devastating reply on the tip of his tongue — who chooses not to say it. That restraint, in that moment, is the real contest, and it is the one that counts with Allah.

A man once came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked for advice. The Prophet ﷺ said: “Do not become angry.” The man asked again, and again the answer was the same: “Do not become angry” (Sahih al-Bukhari 6116). The repetition is the point: not a technique or shortcut, but a discipline. Make “do not become angry” your standing rule, and build your life around it, not around your reactions.

The Sunnah also gives us a practical first move. In a narration in Sahih al-Bukhari, two men were arguing in the presence of the Prophet ﷺ until one man's face reddened with anger. The Prophet ﷺ said: “I know a word which, if he said it, would take away what he is feeling: ‘I seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.’” Seeking refuge — ta‘awwudh — is not a ritual to perform after the damage is done. It is the emergency brake, to be pulled the moment anger rises, before words escape.

And the reward for the harder path is plain: the Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration in Sahih Muslim, that Allah only increases the honour of a servant who forgives (Sahih Muslim 2588). Forgiveness does not lower you; in Allah's measure, it raises you, a promise worth remembering the next time forgiving feels like losing.

4.Why anger does so much damage

Anger is expensive because its costs are paid in the currency we can least afford: relationships. A marriage can absorb years of friction yet break on a handful of cruel sentences spoken in anger. A child remembers the shouting long after forgetting its cause. A friendship can end over one message typed in rage. Words, unlike actions, cannot be undone — only apologised for, and apologies, however sincere, rarely restore things exactly as they were.

Anger also clouds judgement. Decisions made in anger — quitting a job, ending a relationship, cutting off family, sending that message — are the decisions most often regretted in calm moments. Anger narrows the mind to one thought: win this moment. It hides the longer view: live with this person, in this family, in this community, for years to come. The angrier you are, the less you should trust your judgement in that moment.

There is a spiritual cost too. The Prophet ﷺ taught that anger comes from Satan, and a narration reported in Sunan Abi Dawud advises making wudu when angry, since water extinguishes fire. Anger is the state in which we are most likely to say and do things we will answer for. Guarding the moment of anger is guarding the whole day that follows it.

5.Seven practical steps when anger rises

Knowing anger is harmful is not enough; we need a plan for the moment it arrives. The Sunnah gives us one, step by step:

  1. Pause and stay silent. A narration reported in Musnad Ahmad advises: when one of you becomes angry, let him keep silent. Silence is not surrender. It is the space between feeling and action, and in that space, wisdom has a chance to arrive. The first words in anger are almost never the best words.
  2. Seek refuge with Allah. Say A‘udhu billahi min ash-shaytanir rajeem — “I seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.” Say it out loud, deliberately. It names what is happening and invites Allah into the moment before Satan takes it over.
  3. Change your position. A narration in Sunan Abi Dawud advises: if you are angry while standing, sit down; if anger remains, lie down. The physical shift breaks the momentum of rage. It sounds too simple to work, and that is exactly why it works — it interrupts the body's charge before it becomes words.
  4. Make wudu. Cool water on the face and limbs cools more than the skin. As the narration in Sunan Abi Dawud advises, water extinguishes fire, and anger is a fire. Even a few minutes at the tap can be the difference between a regretted outburst and a controlled response.
  5. Leave the scene, briefly. Step out of the room, take a short walk, delay the reply to that message until morning. Distance is not avoidance; it is strategy. Most conflicts look entirely different after an hour, and many look trivial after a night's sleep.
  6. Remember the reward. Recall Quran 3:134 and the promise that Allah loves those who restrain anger and pardon people. When you hold back, you are not losing face — you are earning something no argument can give you: Allah's love and an increase in honour, as Sahih Muslim 2588 promises.
  7. Answer with what is better. When you do respond, respond with the gentleness of Quran 41:34. A calm tone, a fair word, a refusal to match insult for insult. This is not only kinder; it is stronger. It ends the cycle instead of feeding it, and it leaves your dignity intact.

None of these steps requires talent, only one decision, made in advance: when anger comes, I will follow the plan, not the feeling. Practise them on small irritations, so they are ready for the big ones.

6.When anger is right — and how to repair after it

Two honest questions remain. First: is anger ever right? Yes — when it is for Allah's sake, not your ego's. Anger at injustice, at the violation of Allah's limits, at wrong done to the vulnerable, is a sign of a living heart. But even righteous anger must be expressed righteously: with truth, justice, and dignity, never cruelty. The moment it becomes an excuse to humiliate or take revenge, it has stopped being righteous. The test is simple: would you still be angry if the insult had been to someone else, not to you? If not, it is ego, not principle, and it needs the seven steps above.

Second: what if you have already lost your temper? Then repair, quickly and humbly: apologise without excuses — not “you made me angry,” but “I was wrong to speak that way, and I am sorry.” Ask Allah's forgiveness too; the door of repentance is always open. Losing your temper once does not make you a failure; refusing to repair does far more damage than the outburst. True strength, Islam tells us, is not the loudest voice in the room. It is the quiet hand on your own heart, holding it steady when everything in you wants to explode. That strength belongs not to the naturally calm, but to the deliberately trained. Start today, with the next small irritation: pause, seek refuge, stay silent — and discover, as the Prophet ﷺ promised, that the strongest person in any argument is the one who controls himself.
