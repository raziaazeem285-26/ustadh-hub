The Hijrah: A Journey That Changed History

R Razia Azeem · 13 Nov, 2026

Two men, two camels, and a journey of 400 kilometres that changed history. The Hijrah was not an escape — it was a plan, a trust, and a new beginning.

Seerah & Stories

Two men, two camels, and a journey of more than 400 kilometres across the desert of Arabia. The Hijrah — the migration of the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) from Makkah to Madinah in 622 CE — is the event the Muslim calendar itself is counted from. Yet most of us know it only as a story from childhood. Read it in the words of those who were there, and it becomes a masterclass in planning, trust, friendship, and new beginnings.

This article follows the account preserved in Sahih al-Bukhari and the verses of the Quran that remember it — and it is honest about what is certain and what is not.

1.When Makkah Became Unbearable

For thirteen years the Prophet (peace be upon him) had called the people of Makkah to one God, and for thirteen years the Quraysh had answered with mockery, boycott, and violence. His followers were beaten, his family was starved in the valley of Abu Talib, and the Muslims who could leave had already left — some to Abyssinia, others to Yathrib, a city 400 kilometres to the north whose tribes had pledged to protect him.

The Quraysh saw what was happening. If Muhammad left Makkah, he would have a city, an army, and a state. So their leaders met and plotted, as the Quran records: "And [remember, O Muhammad], when those who disbelieved plotted against you to restrain you or kill you or evict you. But they plan, and Allah plans. And Allah is the best of planners" (Quran 8:30). Their plan was murder: a young man from every clan would strike at once, so no single tribe could be blamed.

The Prophet did not run. He waited for permission — the migration was not a panic. The Muslims had been leaving for months, and he stayed until Allah gave him the command. Then he went to Abu Bakr's house at noon, an hour at which he never visited, and told him permission had been given. Abu Bakr wept — not from fear, but from joy at being the companion of this journey. He had already prepared for it: two camels, fed and kept ready for four months against this very day (Sahih al-Bukhari 3905).

2.The Night of the Departure

That night, the assassins were already surrounding his house. The books of seerah report that Ali ibn Abi Talib (may Allah be pleased with him) spent that night in the Prophet's bed, wrapped in his cloak, so the watchers would think the Prophet was still at home. The same reports tell us that as the Prophet slipped out, he cast a handful of dust toward the men and recited: "And We have put before them a barrier and behind them a barrier and covered them, so they do not see" (Quran 36:9) — and walked past them unseen.

He did not go north toward Madinah. He went south, toward Yemen, to a cave on Mount Thawr. Before he left, he gave Ali the deposits the people of Makkah — the very people plotting to kill him — had entrusted to him, with instructions to return them to their owners. Even on the night of his escape, the trust was returned — the reason his enemies still called him al-Amin, the Trustworthy.

At Abu Bakr's house, the preparations were already made. Asma, the daughter of Abu Bakr, tied the bag of provisions with a strip torn from her waist-wrap — and from that day she was known as Dhat an-Nitaqayn, "she of the two belts" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3905).

3.Three Nights in the Cave of Thawr

The Prophet and Abu Bakr hid in a cave near the top of Mount Thawr, south of Makkah, and stayed there for three nights. Abdullah, the son of Abu Bakr, spent each night with them and slipped back into Makkah before dawn with news of Quraysh's plans. Amir ibn Fuhayrah, the shepherd, grazed a flock nearby and brought them milk after dark. Every night, the city that wanted them dead fed them information and milk through two young men.

One day, the searchers stood above the cave. Abu Bakr whispered that if one of them looked down at his feet, he would see them. The Prophet answered: "Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us" (Quran 9:40). As Abu Bakr later reported it: "What do you think of two, the third of whom is Allah?" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3653).

إِذْ يَقُولُ لِصَٰحِبِهِۦ لَا تَحْزَنْ إِنَّ ٱللَّهَ مَعَنَا

Quran 9:40 — "…when he said to his companion, 'Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.'"

The Quran remembers the cave, but it does not name the cave or the companion — the hadith gives both: Thawr, and Abu Bakr. "If you do not aid the Prophet — Allah has already aided him when those who disbelieved had driven him out, the second of two, when they were in the cave" (Quran 9:40).

One honest note: you may have heard that a spider spun a web across the cave's mouth and a dove nested there. Those reports appear in later books of biography; they are not in the Quran, nor in Sahih al-Bukhari or Sahih Muslim, and scholars differ over their strength. This article does not rely on them — two men sat a few feet below their enemies and were not seen, because Allah was with them. That is the miracle the Quran itself records.

4.Suraqah and the Chase

After three nights, the two men set out on the coastal road toward Yathrib, travelling by night and resting by day, guided by a tribesman of Banu al-Dayl who knew the desert paths. The Quraysh had set a reward equal to the blood-money — a hundred camels — for each of the two men, dead or alive (Sahih al-Bukhari 3906).

Suraqah ibn Malik heard riders had been seen on the coast, and rode out alone after them, hoping for the reward. He came close enough to hear the Prophet reciting the Quran — then his horse stumbled and threw him. He mounted again, and the horse's forelegs sank into the ground up to the knees. Suraqah understood. He called out, asking for safety, and promised to turn back anyone else who came after them. The Prophet granted him safety, and wrote him a letter of protection.

Years later, after the Conquest of Makkah, Suraqah came to the Prophet carrying that same letter — and embraced Islam. The man who had ridden out to capture him for a hundred camels became his follower. Allah's plan had worked itself out in a single life: "But they plan, and Allah plans. And Allah is the best of planners" (Quran 8:30).

5.Quba: The First Mosque Built on Piety

On a Monday in Rabi al-Awwal, in September 622 CE, the Prophet arrived at Quba, on the edge of Yathrib — the city that would now be called Madinah. He stayed there for more than ten nights, and the mosque founded there is the one the Quran calls "a mosque founded on righteousness from the first day" (Quran 9:108).

لَّمَسْجِدٌ أُسِّسَ عَلَى ٱلتَّقْوَىٰ مِنْ أَوَّلِ يَوْمٍ أَحَقُّ أَن تَقُومَ فِيهِ

Quran 9:108 — "A mosque founded on righteousness from the first day is more worthy for you to stand in."

Masjid Quba still stands, and the Prophet attached a remarkable reward to it: "Prayer in the Quba Mosque is like an Umrah" (Jami at-Tirmidhi 324, graded Hasan Gharib). He himself used to visit it, riding and on foot, and pray two rak'ahs there (Sahih Muslim 1399) — a mosque built by travellers fresh from the desert, carrying the reward of a pilgrimage, because it was founded on taqwa from its first day.

Then he rode into Madinah, and his camel knelt at a yard owned by two orphan boys. He bought the land for the mosque — refusing it as a gift — and built Masjid Nabawi there, carrying bricks with his own hands. Around that mosque a city grew, and around that city a civilisation. The Quran gave the migrants their charter: "And those who emigrated for [the cause of] Allah after they had been wronged — We will surely settle them in this world in a good place; and the reward of the Hereafter is greater, if only they could know" (Quran 16:41). 6.What the Hijrah Teaches Us Today

The Hijrah is not only history. It is a pattern for every Muslim who has had to leave something behind and start again. Five lessons stand out.

First, plan — then trust. The Prophet did not simply walk into the desert and hope. He had camels fed for four months, a guide who knew the paths, a son bringing news, a shepherd covering the tracks, and a route nobody expected — and then he trusted Allah completely. A narration reported in Jami at-Tirmidhi (2517) captures it in one line: a man asked whether to tie his camel and trust, or leave it loose and trust — and the Prophet said, "Tie it, and trust in Allah." Planning is not the opposite of tawakkul; it is part of it. Our article on tawakkul — trusting Allah after you have done your part explains this balance in full.

Second, choose your companion well. Abu Bakr was chosen not because he was the strongest or the richest, but because he was the truest friend — the one who wept with joy at the chance to go, who walked sometimes ahead of the Prophet and sometimes behind him, and who entered the cave first to check it was safe. "Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us" was spoken to two men — because the journey of faith is never walked alone.

Third, sacrifice comes before the reward. The migrants left homes, businesses, families — everything — and the Quran honours that loss: "And whoever emigrates for the cause of Allah will find on the earth many [alternative] locations and abundance" (Quran 4:100). What they lost in Makkah, Allah replaced in Madinah — not always in the same form, but with abundance.

Fourth, a new beginning is built, not wished for. The first thing the Prophet did in Quba was build a mosque; in Madinah, another. Then he joined the migrants and the helpers of Madinah in brotherhood, one by one — the Ansar who loved those who emigrated to them and preferred them over themselves, "even though they were in privation" (Quran 59:9). A community is not found; it is built, brick by brick, starting with prayer.

"Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us." — Quran 9:40. The shortest sermon of the Hijrah, spoken in a cave, to a frightened friend — and it has carried frightened believers for fourteen centuries.

Fifth, begin with a dua. Before the journey, the Prophet was taught to pray: "My Lord, cause me to enter a sound entrance and to exit a sound exit and grant me from Yourself a supporting authority" (Quran 17:80). It is the dua for every departure and arrival.

Fourteen centuries later, the Muslim calendar still begins with this journey — not with a battle, not with a conquest, but with a migration. Allah did not date our history from a victory, but from the day His Prophet left everything behind, trusting Him. Your own new beginning may not need a desert or a cave. It needs the same two things the Hijrah had: a plan, and the words, "Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us."
