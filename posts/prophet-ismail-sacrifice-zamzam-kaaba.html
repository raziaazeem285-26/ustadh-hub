Prophet Ismail (AS): The Sacrifice, the Zamzam, and the Kaaba

R Razia Azeem · 15 Nov, 2026

A father told to sacrifice his son, a mother running between two hills for water, and a boy who helped raise the House of Allah — the story of Ismail, the prophet of patience and promise.

In this article
  1. A Son Long Prayed For
  2. Left in a Valley Without Crops
  3. Seven Runs Between Safa and Marwa
  4. The Dream and the Great Sacrifice
  5. Father and Son Raise the House
  6. True to His Promise

Seerah & Stories

Every Muslim knows his name from Hajj. The water pilgrims drink is the water his mother found. The hills they run between are the hills she ran between. The House they circle is the House he helped his father raise. And the sacrifice they remember every Eid al-Adha is the sacrifice Allah ransomed him from. His name is Ismail, son of Ibrahim — and his story is one of the most complete pictures in the Quran of what it means to submit to Allah: as a son, as a husband, as a father, and as a prophet.

1.A Son Long Prayed For

Ibrahim had waited a lifetime for a son. He had left his homeland, been thrown into a fire for his faith, and migrated for Allah's sake — and still he had no child. So he prayed the prayer the Quran records for every couple still waiting: "My Lord, grant me [a child] from among the righteous" (Quran 37:100). Allah answered him with "good tidings of a forbearing boy" (Quran 37:101) — a gentle, patient son. That boy was Ismail, born to Hajar. His mother's name is not given in the Quran; it comes to us from the narrations of the Prophet (peace be upon him).

Years later, with two sons beside him, Ibrahim would look back and say: "Praise to Allah, who has granted to me in old age Ishmael and Isaac. Indeed, my Lord is the Hearer of supplication" (Quran 14:39). The Quran gives Ismail a place of his own among the prophets: "And [mention] Ishmael and Idris and Dhul-Kifl; all were of the patient" (Quran 21:85). Patience is the first word the Quran attaches to him — and the whole of his story explains why. 2.Left in a Valley Without Crops

When Ismail was still a nursing baby, Allah commanded Ibrahim to take Hajar and her son to a barren valley in Arabia — the place where Makkah stands today. The long narration reported by Ibn Abbas in Sahih al-Bukhari (3364) tells the story in full. Ibrahim seated them under a large tree, near where the well of Zamzam would later appear, in the upper part of what is now the Haram. At that time there was no one in Makkah, and there was no water there. He left them a leather bag of dates and a waterskin of water — and he turned to leave.

Hajar followed him. "O Ibrahim, where are you going, leaving us in this valley where there are no people and nothing at all?" She asked him again and again, and he did not turn towards her. Then she asked the question that changed everything: "Is it Allah who commanded you to do this?" He said, "Yes." And she answered with words that mothers have repeated for four thousand years: "Then He will not let us be lost."

Ibrahim walked on until he reached a pass where they could no longer see him. Then he turned towards the place of the House, raised his hands, and prayed the dua the Quran preserves: "Our Lord, I have settled some of my offspring in a valley without cultivation, near Your sacred House, our Lord, that they may establish prayer. So make hearts among the people incline toward them and provide for them from the fruits that they might be grateful" (Quran 14:37). He left his wife and his baby in a desert with no water — because the One who had commanded it had also promised to provide.

3.Seven Runs Between Safa and Marwa

Hajar nursed her child and drank from the waterskin until the water ran out. Then she grew thirsty, and her son grew thirsty, and she could not bear to watch him writhing with thirst. The narration says she found Safa, the nearest hill, and stood on it, looking for anyone — but she saw no one. She came down, crossed the valley, lifting the edge of her garment and running as a person in distress runs, and climbed Marwa and looked again. She did that seven times.

On the seventh time she heard a voice. She called out, "Whoever you are, help me if you can help," and there, at the place of Zamzam, she found an angel, who dug with his heel — or his wing — until water appeared. She began making a basin around it with her hands, scooping water into her waterskin while it kept gushing up after every scoop. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said: "May Allah have mercy on the mother of Ismail. Had she left Zamzam — or had she not scooped from the water — Zamzam would have been a flowing spring" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3364). And the angel said to her: "Do not fear being lost, for here is the House of Allah. This boy and his father will build it, and Allah does not let His people be lost."

The water drew life to the valley. A tribe passing by — Jurhum — saw birds circling and knew there was water, and they asked Hajar's permission to settle there. She agreed, and Ismail grew up among them, learning Arabic, and when he was grown he married from among them. The running between Safa and Marwa that pilgrims still perform in Hajj and Umrah commemorates her search: a mother's desperate run between two hills, turned by Allah into an act of worship for every generation after her.

4.The Dream and the Great Sacrifice

Years passed, and the boy grew until he was old enough to walk and work beside his father. Then Ibrahim saw a dream — and the dream of a prophet is a command. "O my dear son," he said, "I have seen in a dream that I must sacrifice you. So look, what do you think?"

فَلَمَّا بَلَغَ مَعَهُ السَّعْيَ قَالَ يَا بُنَيَّ إِنِّي أَرَىٰ فِي الْمَنَامِ أَنِّي أَذْبَحُكَ فَانظُرْ مَاذَا تَرَىٰ ۚ قَالَ يَا أَبَتِ افْعَلْ مَا تُؤْمَرُ ۖ سَتَجِدُنِي إِن شَاءَ اللَّهُ مِنَ الصَّابِرِينَ

Quran 37:102 — "And when he reached with him [the age of] exertion, he said, 'O my son, indeed I have seen in a dream that I [must] sacrifice you, so see what you think.' He said, 'O my father, do as you are commanded. You will find me, if Allah wills, of the steadfast.'"

No argument. No delay. No bargaining with the command. The son's answer is the same word the Quran attached to him from the beginning: patience. "You will find me, if Allah wills, of the steadfast." Then the Quran says: "And when they had both submitted and he put him down upon his forehead, We called to him, 'O Abraham, you have fulfilled the vision.' Indeed, We thus reward the doers of good. Indeed, this was the clear trial. And We ransomed him with a great sacrifice" (Quran 37:103-107).

Two things are worth stating plainly. First, the Quran does not name the son in these verses. The majority of scholars hold that it was Ismail — the sequence of the surah supports it, because the glad tidings of Isaac's birth come only after the sacrifice, in the very next passage (Quran 37:112-113), and Isaac had not yet been born. Second, the Quran gives no date or age for the trial. What it does record is the submission of both of them — father and son, together — and the ransom that followed. Every Eid al-Adha, when Muslims sacrifice an animal and remember Ibrahim's test, they are remembering this moment: a father who obeyed, a son who submitted, and a Lord who never intended the boy's death, only the proof of their faith. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) himself was called "the son of the two sacrificed ones" — Ismail, and his own father Abdullah — and when a Bedouin addressed him so, he smiled and did not deny the description (reported in al-Hakim's al-Mustadrak).

5.Father and Son Raise the House

The angel's promise to Hajar came true. When Ismail was grown, Ibrahim returned, and father and son set about the work together. The narration in Sahih al-Bukhari (3364) tells us Ismail was sitting under a tree near Zamzam, straightening his arrows, when his father came and said: "O Ismail, Allah has commanded me to do something." "Do what your Lord has commanded you to do," his son replied. "And will you help me?" Ibrahim asked. "I will help you," he said. And together they raised the foundations of the House of Allah.

وَإِذْ يَرْفَعُ إِبْرَاهِيمُ الْقَوَاعِدَ مِنَ الْبَيْتِ وَإِسْمَاعِيلُ رَبَّنَا تَقَبَّلْ مِنَّا ۖ إِنَّكَ أَنتَ السَّمِيعُ الْعَلِيمُ

Quran 2:127 — "And [mention] when Abraham was raising the foundations of the House and [with him] Ishmael, [saying], 'Our Lord, accept [this] from us. Indeed, You are the Hearing, the Knowing.'"

They built it stone by stone, and as they built, they prayed — not for reward, not for recognition, but for acceptance: "Our Lord, accept this from us." The Quran also records the rest of their dua: make this a place of security, provide its people with fruits, raise up among them a messenger who will recite Allah's verses and teach them the Book and wisdom, and show us our rites and accept our repentance (Quran 2:125-129). Every prayer a Muslim prays facing that House, every Hajj that circles it, every drop of Zamzam drunk beside it — all of it traces back to a father and a son building together in a valley that once had no water and no people. "O my father, do as you are commanded. You will find me, if Allah wills, of the steadfast." — Quran 37:102. A son's answer to the hardest command a father ever had to give — and the sentence that made his name a byword for patience.

6.True to His Promise

The Quran sums Ismail up in two verses that say more than whole books could. "And mention in the Book, Ishmael. Indeed, he was true to his promise, and he was a messenger and a prophet. And he used to enjoin upon his family prayer and zakah and was to his Lord pleasing" (Quran 19:54-55). True to his promise — the promise he made as a boy on the day of the sacrifice, kept for a lifetime. And his first care, after his own worship, was his family: he told them to pray, and he told them to give. A prophet whose mission began at home.

Five lessons stand out. First, Allah provides from where you cannot see: a well in a waterless valley, opened by a mother's running and a baby's feet. Second, patience is not passive: Hajar ran between the hills, Ismail helped raise the House, and both of them acted while they trusted. Third, the hardest test can be the greatest honour: the sacrifice that was never completed became the memory of every Eid. Fourth, build with your children, not just for them: Ibrahim did not hand his son a finished House — he handed him a stone and said, help me. Fifth, keep your word: the Quran's praise of Ismail is not his miracles or his kingdom — it is that he was true to his promise.

Ismail was left as a baby in a desert, ran the hills as a boy for water, stood ready as a son for sacrifice, and built as a man the House that the whole world still faces. His story begins with a mother's words — "Then He will not let us be lost" — and ends with a son's words — "You will find me, if Allah wills, of the steadfast." Between those two sentences is the whole of a prophet's life.
