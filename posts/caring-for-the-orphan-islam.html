Caring for the Orphan: Close to the Prophet ط in Paradise

R Razia Azeem · 5 Nov, 2026

In every community there are children growing up without a father's protection, and Islam places them at the very centre of its social conscience. The Quran returns to the orphan again and again — not as a side note, but as a test of a society's heart. And the reward for caring for one is unlike almost any other: a place beside the Prophet ط himself in Paradise.

Duas & Fazilat

Some children in your street, your extended family, or your masjid community are growing up without a father. Their mother may be doing everything she can, but there is a gap no single person can easily fill: protection, provision, guidance, and the simple feeling of being looked after. Islam noticed that gap fourteen centuries ago, and it has never stopped talking about it.

This is not a small topic in our faith. The Quran mentions orphans in verse after verse, in Makkah and in Madinah, in commands about wealth, worship, and the Day of Judgement itself. How a community treats its orphans is, in the Quran's picture, one of the clearest signs of whether that community truly believes. This article looks at what Allah commands, what the Prophet ط promised, and what you and I can actually do about it this week.

1.Who is an orphan in Islam?

In Islamic teaching, a yateem is a child who has lost his or her father before reaching adulthood. The loss of the father is the defining point, because in the society the Quran addressed, the father was the provider and protector; a child without one was exposed to poverty, neglect, and exploitation. The definition is not about feeling sorry for a child — it is about recognising a real vulnerability and assigning the whole community a duty to cover it.

That duty begins with a remarkable fact: the Prophet Muhammad ط himself was an orphan. His father passed away before he was born, and his mother passed away when he was only six years old. He was raised first by his grandfather and then by his uncle. Allah reminds him of this in Surah Ad-Duha:

أَلَمْ يَجِدْكَ يَتِيمًا فَآَاوَىٰ

Quran 93:6 — “Did He not find you an orphan and give you shelter?”

The One who was Himself an orphan became the greatest protector of orphans. That is not a coincidence. It tells us that caring for orphans is not charity from a distance; it is personal to the heart of this religion.

2.What the Quran commands about orphans

The Quran's commands about orphans are strikingly direct. In Surah Ad-Duha, right after reminding the Prophet ط that he was given shelter as an orphan, Allah says: “As for the orphan, do not oppress him” (Quran 93:9). The verse does not say “be nice when you remember”; it forbids harshness, humiliation, and pushing the orphan aside.

Surah Al-Ma'un goes further, and makes it a matter of faith itself. It describes the one who denies the Day of Judgement as the one who “repulses the orphan” (Quran 107:2). In other words, turning an orphan away from your door is presented as evidence that the Hereafter has not really entered your heart. And Surah Al-Fajr lists, among the failures of a heedless society, that “you do not honour the orphan” (Quran 89:17).

But the Quran does not only warn; it also guides. When the companions asked about orphans, the answer came: “Improving their condition is best” (Quran 2:220, in meaning). The goal is not a one-time handout that leaves the child exactly where he was. The goal is to genuinely improve the child's life — his food, his schooling, his upbringing, his future.

3.The promise: beside the Prophet in Paradise

Then comes the promise that stops every listener in their tracks. The Prophet ط said, in a narration reported in Sahih al-Bukhari (6005):

“I and the person who looks after an orphan and provides for him, will be in Paradise like this,” putting his index and middle fingers together.

Two fingers, side by side, with almost no gap between them. That is the picture of closeness being offered — not just entry into Paradise, but nearness to the Prophet ط himself. Scholars have noted that this reward is given for kafalah: taking responsibility for an orphan's care and provision, whether the child is related to you or not, whether you raise the child in your own home or support the child from your own wealth.

A second narration widens the circle. The Prophet ط said that the one who looks after a widow or a poor person is like a warrior striving in the path of Allah, or like one who prays through the night and fasts through the day (Sahih al-Bukhari 5353, in meaning). Much of the time, caring for an orphan and caring for a widowed mother go together; the reward described here covers both.

4.The warning: never touch an orphan's wealth

The Quran pairs its encouragement with one of its sternest warnings. “Indeed, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming fire into their bellies” (Quran 4:10, in meaning). An orphan's inheritance, money, or property is a trust, and taking from it — even quietly, even through a relative's authority — is treated as one of the gravest wrongs.

The same surah gives the practical rule: test orphans as they grow, and when they reach maturity and show sound judgement, hand their property over to them, fully and fairly (Quran 4:6, in meaning). Guardianship is therefore not ownership. It is a temporary trust with an end date, and the guardian will answer for it. Anyone who holds an orphan's money, manages an orphan's affairs, or administers a fund in an orphan's name should read these verses as if they were addressed to him personally — because they are.

5.What real care looks like

So what does caring for an orphan actually mean in daily life? It is wider than writing a cheque, though money matters. Real care has several faces:

Provision without humiliation. Food, clothing, school fees, and medical care, given in a way that protects the child's dignity. The child should never be made to feel like a burden, or reminded of what was given. The Quran's word is honour (89:17) — and honour shows in tone as much as in amount.

Presence, not just payment. A child who has lost a father needs more than funds; he needs adults who show up. A regular visit, help with homework, a place at your table, someone who asks how school went. Sponsorship from far away is valuable, but a child also needs to be known by name in his own community.

Upbringing and education. The best care prepares the child for independence: schooling, a skill, good company, and Islamic upbringing. The Quran's standard is “improving their condition” (2:220) — so the question is always whether the child is better off this year than last year.

Justice in the family. Sometimes the hardest cases are inside the family: an orphan's inheritance delayed, a property used by relatives, a child passed between homes. Standing up for the orphan's rights within your own family may be the most difficult — and the most rewarded — form of this duty. For a related reflection on keeping trusts, read Amanah: The Trust in Your Hands, and on giving that multiplies blessing, The Virtue of Charity.

6.What you can do this week

Start close to home. Ask your family, quietly, whether there is an orphaned child among your relatives or neighbours whose situation you do not fully know. If there is, find out one real need — school fees, winter clothing, a tutor, a mentor — and commit to it regularly rather than once. Regularity is what turns a donation into care.

Second, if you support orphans through an organisation, choose one you can verify: clear reporting, named children or projects, and a record you can check. The duty of guarding the orphan's wealth applies to donors too — your sadaqah should reach the child it was meant for.

Third, include orphans in the ordinary life of your home and masjid: invite them, remember them at Eid, and make sure the widowed mother is not left to carry everything alone. A community where no orphan feels invisible is a community that has understood Surah Ad-Duha.

Finally, teach your own children. Let them share, let them befriend, let them learn that the child without a father is not an object of pity but a neighbour with rights. The Prophet ط was an orphan, and the best of this ummah have always measured themselves by how they treated its orphans. That measure is still open to us today — and the reward, two fingers side by side in Paradise, is still on offer.
