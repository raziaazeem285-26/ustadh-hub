Justice (Adl): Standing Firm for Truth, Even Against Yourself

R Razia Azeem · 29 Oct, 2026

Most people love justice when they are the ones wronged. Islam asks something harder: love justice when it costs you — when the truth favours the other side, when the person in the wrong is your own family, when the one you must judge fairly is someone you dislike. That is adl, and Allah does not present it as an optional virtue. He commands it.

Most people love justice when they are the ones wronged. Islam asks something harder: love justice when it costs you — when the truth favours the other side, when the person in the wrong is your own family, when the one you must judge fairly is someone you dislike. That is adl, and Allah does not present it as an optional virtue. He commands it.

Justice is one of the great themes of this blog's virtue series, and it belongs beside truthfulness, mercy and forgiveness — but it is not the same as any of them. Truthfulness is about what you say. Mercy is about how gently you treat people. Justice is about what each person is owed, and giving it whether you feel like it or not. A person can be kind and still unjust: kind to his own circle, harsh to outsiders. Adl corrects exactly that imbalance.

1. What Adl Really Means

The Arabic word adl comes from a root meaning to straighten, to balance, to set something in its right place. The image is a scale in perfect balance — not tilted toward the rich, not tilted toward the poor, not tilted toward your own side. Justice, in Islam, is giving every person and every thing its due: Allah His due, people their due, your own soul its due. It is not the same as equality in every case — a parent does not give a toddler and a teenager the same freedoms — but it is always fairness: the same standard, applied to everyone, including yourself.

That is why justice is listed among the names and attributes of Allah Himself: He is Al-Adl, the Just. On the Day of Judgement, the Quran says, no soul will be wronged in the least — not even by the weight of an atom (Quran 4:40, in meaning). If Allah's own judgement is perfectly just, then a believer's judgement should aim in that direction — every dispute settled, every story judged, every opinion formed is a small version of it.

2. Allah Commands Justice, Not Just Kindness

Justice is not a suggestion in the Quran; it is a command, repeated in the strongest terms. "Indeed, Allah commands justice and good conduct," He says (Quran 16:90, in meaning). Notice the pairing: justice first, then kindness. Kindness without justice becomes favouritism — you are kind to the people you like and forget the rest. Justice without kindness becomes cold — you give everyone their due but never an extra smile. Islam wants both, in that order: first be fair, then be generous on top of fairness.

The command is built into the very purpose of revelation. "We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and with them the Book and the Balance, so that people may uphold justice" (Quran 57:25, in meaning). The Balance — the scale — is mentioned alongside the Book itself. Revelation did not come only to teach prayer and fasting; it came to straighten the scales of human life. And when you are entrusted with judging between people, the command is direct: "Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due, and when you judge between people, to judge with justice" (Quran 4:58, in meaning). A trust is not only money: a reputation, a secret, a child's future are trusts too, and justice returns each one intact.

"O you who believe, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives." — Quran 4:135

3. Even Against Yourself

Here is where the Quran raises the bar beyond anything a human court asks. "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives. Whether one is rich or poor, Allah is more worthy of both. So follow not personal inclination, lest you not be just" (Quran 4:135). Read that slowly. Justice is not only for the courtroom, and not only against strangers. It is for the moment when admitting the truth will cost you money, or embarrass you, or disappoint your family — and you admit it anyway, because you are a witness for Allah, not for your own side.

This is the hardest test of adl, and the most common one. It appears when two children fight and your own child started it. It appears when you promised something and keeping the promise now hurts. It appears when you are wrong in an argument and everyone is waiting for you to say so. Most people in those moments do not lie outright — they soften, delay, change the subject, blame the other side a little. The Quran calls that, too, a failure of justice: "if you distort your testimony or refuse to give it, then indeed Allah is ever, with what you do, Acquainted" (Quran 4:135, continued). Allah sees the softening as clearly as He sees the lie.

4. Even Against People You Dislike

The second hard test is the mirror image of the first. "O you who believe, be steadfast for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness" (Quran 5:8, in meaning). It is easy to be fair to people you admire. The command is to be fair to people you do not like — people who hurt you, people whose views anger you, people you would rather see lose.

Think how often this test arrives in ordinary life. You hear a story about someone you dislike and pass it on without checking — because it feels true, because it fits what you already believe. That is injustice, even if the story happens to be true, because you denied them the fairness of verification. You praise your friend's mistake as a misunderstanding and condemn your enemy's misunderstanding as a crime. That, too, is injustice — and the Quran names its cure in one line: be just, for that is nearer to righteousness. Not nearer to winning. Nearer to righteousness.

Notice what this verse does not say. It does not say you must like everyone, or agree with everyone, or pretend dislike does not exist. Dislike is a feeling; justice is an action. You may dislike a person and still give them their full due — their fair hearing, their honest wage, their good name, their share. That separation, between what you feel and what you do, is the very heart of adl.

5. The Prophet's Justice Had No Favourites

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ lived this command in a way his companions never forgot. Aishah (RA) reported that the Quraysh were deeply worried about the case of a woman from the tribe of Makhzum who had committed theft. They hoped someone would intercede for her with the Prophet ﷺ, and they chose Usamah ibn Zayd (RA), the Prophet's beloved companion, to speak to him. When Usamah raised the matter, the Prophet ﷺ asked him, in meaning: "Do you intercede regarding one of the punishments prescribed by Allah?" Then he stood and addressed the people: the nations before you were ruined, he said, because when a noble person among them stole, they let him go, but when a weak person stole, they punished him. "By Allah, were Fatimah, the daughter of Muhammad, to steal, I would cut off her hand" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3475, in meaning).

That sentence is the whole of adl in one line. No tribe, no rank, no closeness to the Prophet himself — not even his own daughter, named only to make the point absolute — could bend the scale. The nations before were ruined, he said, not by their enemies but by their double standard: one law for the noble, another for the weak. Every person who practises two standards in his own small world — one rule for his children, another for others' children; one excuse for himself, another judgement for everyone else — is walking the same road.

The reward for the opposite path is just as striking. Abu Hurayrah (RA) reported that the Prophet ﷺ said that seven kinds of people will be shaded by Allah on the Day when there is no shade but His — and the first he named was "a just ruler" (Sahih al-Bukhari 660; Sahih Muslim 1031). Before the youth raised in worship, before the heart attached to the mosque, before the secret giver of charity: the just one. Because justice is the virtue that protects everyone else's virtues. Without it, worship becomes a private comfort and charity becomes a public show. With it, a whole community can breathe.

6. Justice Begins in Small Places

Most of us will never sit on a judge's bench or rule a country. That does not remove us from the command — it relocates it. Justice for most people lives in small rooms: between husband and wife, between parent and children, between employer and worker, between friends, between siblings over an inheritance, between neighbours over a boundary. The just ruler of the hadith is the headline; the just parent, the just employer, the just friend are the daily edition of the same virtue.

Ask yourself three small questions, and you will see how much of adl is within your reach today. First, in your home: do all your children feel they get the same fairness from you — the same listening, the same rules, the same apology when you are wrong? Second, at work: do you pay on time, measure honestly, and keep promises even when the other person cannot force you to? Third, in speech: when you repeat a story, have you checked it, and would you say it the same way if that person were standing in front of you?

And justice includes justice to yourself. Your body has a right over you: rest, food, health. Your soul has a right: prayer, learning, quiet. A person who works himself into illness, or neglects his family for ambition, or starves his heart of worship for his career, is unjust to the trust Allah placed in his own hands. Adl is balance in every direction, including inward.

7. Five Daily Ways to Practise Adl

Justice, like every virtue in this series, is trained by small daily acts. Try these five this week.

1. Admit one thing against yourself each day. When you are wrong — in a discussion, in a decision, in a promise — say so plainly, without softening it.

2. Verify before you repeat. Before passing on any story about a person, especially someone you dislike, ask: do I know this is true, or do I only wish it were? If you cannot verify it, do not carry it. That one habit would clean up half the injustice in any community.

3. Give the other side a fair hearing — out loud. In a family dispute, state the other person's case as fairly as you can before answering it. You will often discover the dispute was smaller than it looked, and you will always discover whether you were being fair.

4. Keep one promise that costs you. Pay on time when it is inconvenient. Return what you borrowed when you would rather keep it. Honour the agreement when the other person cannot enforce it. Justice is proven exactly where enforcement ends.

5. End the day with a scale-check. Before sleep, ask: today, did anyone receive less than their due from me — a child, a spouse, a worker, a stranger, my own soul? If so, make it right tomorrow, starting with an apology. The scale is balanced daily, by people willing to adjust it.

Justice is not the virtue of judges and rulers alone. It is the virtue of everyone who has ever had to choose between what is easy and what is right — which is to say, everyone. Allah commands it, the Prophet ﷺ modelled it without favourites, and its reward begins with a shade on a Day when shade will be the rarest thing in creation. Stand firm for truth today, even against yourself, even for people you dislike — and trust that the scale you straighten for others is the scale Allah will straighten for you.
