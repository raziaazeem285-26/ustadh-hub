Prophet Ayyub (AS): Patience That Never Broke

R Razia Azeem · 10 Nov, 2026

When everything was taken from him — his wealth, his family, his health — Prophet Ayyub (AS) turned to Allah with one short dua, and Allah called him an excellent servant. Here is his story from the Quran, and what it teaches us when hardship comes.

Seerah & Stories

In Urdu and Persian, there is a proverb that every household knows: Sabr-e-Ayyub — the patience of Job. When someone bears a long illness, a heavy loss, or a trial that will not end, people say, "He has the patience of Ayyub." The name of Prophet Ayyub (AS) has become the very measure of patience. Yet most of us know the proverb better than the story. The Quran tells his story in only a few verses, in two short passages — and those few verses carry everything a person in hardship needs to hear.

This is not a story about a man who never felt pain. Ayyub (AS) felt it fully — but his pain never broke his trust, and his trust was answered with a mercy greater than what he had lost.

1.Who was Prophet Ayyub (AS)?

Prophet Ayyub (AS) is named in the Quran among the rightly guided prophets. Allah says, "And We gave him Isaac and Jacob — all [of them] We guided. And Noah, We guided before; and among his descendants, David and Solomon and Job and Joseph and Moses and Aaron. Thus do We reward the doers of good" (Quran 6:84, in meaning). Revelation came to him just as it came to Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa and Isa: "Indeed, We have revealed to you, [O Muhammad], as We revealed to Noah and the prophets after him. And We revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, the Descendants, Jesus, Job, Jonah, Aaron, and Solomon" (Quran 4:163, in meaning).

The Quran gives us no birthplace, no date, and no list of his wealth — and we should be honest about that. His story in the Quran is not a biography; it is a lesson. It comes in two passages. In Surah Al-Anbiya, Allah shows us his prayer. In Surah Sad, Allah shows us his trial, his cure, and the verdict on his life. Everything we need is in those verses; where the Quran is silent, we do not invent details.

2.The trial: what the Quran actually says

In Surah Sad, Ayyub (AS) describes his own condition to his Lord: "Indeed, Satan has touched me with hardship and torment" (Quran 38:41, in meaning). Notice how he speaks: he attributes the affliction to Satan's touch, not to any injustice from Allah. He does not say, "You have wronged me." He states what has happened to him, and he turns to the only One who can remove it.

The classical commentators describe a trial that touched everything a person can lose — his wealth, his children, and his health — and that people kept away from him, except his wife. The Quran itself does not fix the number of years the trial lasted — the commentators mention differing reports, some saying thirteen years, some seventeen, some eighteen — and we should not present any number as certain when the Quran does not give one. What is certain is the picture the Quran paints: a man who had everything, then lost everything, and still did not lose Allah.

That is the first lesson of Ayyub (AS): a trial is not proof that Allah has abandoned you. The prophets — the best of people — were tested the most. The Prophet ﷺ said, when asked which people are most severely tested, "The Prophets, then the next best and the next best. A person is tested according to his religious commitment" (Sunan Ibn Majah 4023, hasan). And he said, "Trials will continue to afflict a person until they leave him walking on the earth with no sin on him" (Jami at-Tirmidhi 2398, sahih). Ayyub's trial was not a punishment. It was the honour of a man Allah trusted with a heavy test.

3.The dua: one short sentence

After years of hardship, Ayyub (AS) finally called upon his Lord. His entire prayer, as the Quran records it, is a single short sentence:

أَنِّي مَسَّنِيَ الضُّرُّ وَأَنتَ أَرْحَمُ الرَّاحِمِينَ

Quran 21:83

"Indeed, adversity has touched me, and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful" (Quran 21:83, in meaning). There is no complaint in it. There is no demand. He does not list what he has lost, and he does not ask "why me?" He simply states his condition — adversity has touched me — and then he affirms who Allah is — the Most Merciful of the merciful. That is the whole prayer.

Look at the manners of this dua, because they teach us how to pray in our own hardships. First, he complained to Allah, not about Allah. There is a difference between telling Allah that you are hurting and accusing Him of being unfair, and Ayyub (AS) never crossed that line. Second, he did not instruct Allah on how to fix it. He left the how to Allah and held on to the who — the Most Merciful. Third, he prayed after enduring for so long without giving up — his patience did not mean he never asked. Patience is not silence; patience is asking while trusting.

This is a dua worth memorising word for word — short enough for a child to learn, deep enough for a lifetime. When illness comes, when a door closes, when a loss sits heavy on the chest, these are the words to reach for — the same words a prophet used when he had nothing left. It is the same pattern as the dua of Prophet Yunus (AS) in the darkness of the whale, which we wrote about in Yunus's Dua: Answered in the Darkest Place: a short, honest call, and an answer that came from Allah alone.

4.The answer: "So We responded to him"

The very next verse gives the answer: "So We responded to him and removed what afflicted him of adversity. And We gave him [back] his family and the like thereof with them as mercy from Us and a reminder for the worshippers [of Allah]" (Quran 21:84, in meaning). Allah did not only remove the affliction — He returned the family, and doubled it. The loss was not simply undone; it was replaced with more than had been taken. That is how Allah answers patience: not with the minimum, but with a mercy that overflows.

Surah Sad adds the detail of how the cure came. Allah told him, "Strike [the ground] with your foot; this is a [spring for] a cool bath and drink" (Quran 38:42, in meaning). Notice that Allah gave him something to do: the cure was a miracle, a spring at his foot, but it still came through an action — he had to strike the ground. That is the pattern of tawakkul in the Quran: take the means Allah puts in front of you, and trust Allah for the result. We take our medicine, we seek help, we do our part — and we know the healing itself belongs to Allah alone.

Then the passage mentions an oath. "And take in your hand a bunch [of grass] and strike with it and do not break your oath" (Quran 38:44, in meaning). In his hardship, Ayyub (AS) had sworn an oath concerning his wife, and Allah showed him a way to fulfil it without harm — one light strike with a bunch of stalks instead of a severe one. Even in a great trial, the small duties still matter, and Allah Himself makes a way to keep them with gentleness.

"Indeed, We found him patient, an excellent servant. Indeed, he was one repeatedly turning back [to Allah]." — Quran 38:44, in meaning

That is Allah's own verdict on his life. Not "a rich man" or "a healthy man" — an excellent servant. And the reason given is twofold: he was patient, and he kept turning back to Allah. Patience and repentance, held together, are what made him excellent.

5.The golden locusts: gratitude after the trial

There is one more scene in the story, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari, and it shows the same heart in plenty that the trial had shown in hardship. After his recovery, while Ayyub (AS) was bathing, golden locusts began to fall upon him, and he started gathering them in his clothes. Allah called to him, "O Job! Have I not made you rich?" He replied, "Yes, my Lord, but I cannot do without Your blessing" (Sahih al-Bukhari 3391, in meaning).

Think about that answer. He was not gathering the gold out of greed; he could not bear to let a single blessing of Allah fall unused. The man who had lost everything without complaint was the same man who received everything with gratitude. That is the full picture of Ayyub (AS): patient in loss, grateful in gain, and turning back to Allah in both. Patience and gratitude are not two different virtues. They are the same heart, facing two different seasons.

6.Five lessons from Ayyub for our own hardships

1. A trial is not a sign that Allah has left you. The prophets were tested the most, and Ayyub (AS) most of all among them in his own way. If your life is hard right now, it does not mean Allah is angry with you — it may mean He is drawing you closer. "Do the people think that they will be left to say, 'We believe' and they will not be tried?" (Quran 29:2, in meaning). The trial is part of the road, not a sign that you have left it.

2. No pain is ever wasted. The Prophet ﷺ said, "No fatigue, nor disease, nor sorrow, nor sadness, nor hurt, nor distress befalls a Muslim, even if it were the prick he receives from a thorn, but that Allah expiates some of his sins for that" (Sahih al-Bukhari 5641, in meaning). Every ache, every sleepless night — borne with patience, none of it is lost. It is counted.

3. Complain to Allah, not about Allah. Learn Ayyub's dua and use it: "Indeed, adversity has touched me, and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful." Say it in your own words too: Allah already knows your pain, and telling Him is not weakness — it is worship. What breaks a person is not the complaint; it is the belief that no one is listening.

4. Take the means, then trust. Ayyub (AS) struck the ground, and the spring appeared. Your part might be a doctor's appointment, a phone call, or an apology. Do it, then leave the result with Allah. "And whoever relies upon Allah — then He is sufficient for him" (Quran 65:3, in meaning). For more on this balance of effort and trust, read Tawakkul: Trusting Allah After You Have Done Your Part.

5. Relief has a time — and it will come. "For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease. Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease" (Quran 94:5-6, in meaning). Allah repeated the promise twice in one breath. Ayyub's answer came after his dua, not before it, and after years, not days. If your answer has not come yet, it does not mean it is not coming. Keep calling, the way he did — and when it comes, be ready to be grateful, the way he was.

Ayyub (AS) lost his wealth, his children, and his health — and kept the one thing that mattered: his connection with Allah. That is why his name became the measure of patience. If you are in a hard season today, borrow his words: say them slowly, mean them fully, and wait for the answer the way he waited — knowing the Most Merciful is listening.
