The Two Gardens: When Wealth Becomes a Test of Faith

R Razia Azeem · 20 Nov, 2026

On Fridays Muslims open Surah Al-Kahf, and the surah's second story is a parable about wealth: two gardens, one boast, and a ruin that came after a clear warning. Here is what Quran 18:32-44 teaches about the blessing that became a test.

Quran

On Fridays Muslims across the world open Surah Al-Kahf. The surah holds four stories, each a test of a different kind: faith, wealth, knowledge, and power. This morning's article told the first story — the young believers who slept in a cave for 309 years. This is the second: the parable of the two gardens, in Quran 18:32-44 — the story that speaks most directly to the life most of us live, the life of earning, owning, comparing, and forgetting.

1.A Parable on a Friday

Surah Al-Kahf is the 18th surah of the Quran. It has 110 verses, and its four stories are its spine: the Sleepers of the Cave (18:9-26), the Two Gardens (18:32-44), Musa's journey with the servant taught by Allah (18:60-82), and Dhul-Qarnayn (18:83-98). The surah states the purpose of such tests at the start: "Indeed, We have made that which is on the earth adornment for it that We may test them [as to] which of them is best in deed." (Quran 18:7)

The story opens with a command: "And set forth to them the parable of two men: for one of them We made two gardens of grapevines, and We surrounded them with date-palms, and between them We made cornfields." (Quran 18:32) One thing should be said plainly first. The Quran names neither man and gives no place or date. It calls the story a parable — a mathal, an example set forth to teach — and the lesson does not depend on their identities. What matters is the pattern: a blessing, a boast, a warning, and a ruin.

2.Two Gardens, One Boast

The gift is described with care, because its size is part of the test. Two gardens of grapevines, ringed with date-palms, fields of crops between them, a river flowing through the middle. "Both gardens yielded their fruit, and failed not in the least, and We caused a river to gush forth in their midst." (Quran 18:33) Nothing failed. Nothing was withheld. The man had fruit in abundance — and a companion with far less.

Then he spoke, and the first thing he said was a comparison:

وَكَانَ لَهُۥ ثَمَرٌۭ فَقَالَ لِصَٰحِبِهِۦ وَهُوَ يُحَاوِرُهُۥٓ أَنَا۠ أَكْثَرُ مِنكَ مَالًۭا وَأَعَزُّ نَفَرًۭا

Quran 18:34

"And he had fruit, so he said to his companion while he was conversing with him: 'I am greater than you in wealth and mightier in followers.'" The blessing had barely been described before it was turned into a weapon. The gardens were no longer a gift to be grateful for; they were evidence in an argument, and the measure was money and men.

Then he entered his garden — and the Quran adds a phrase that should stop every reader: "while he was unjust to himself" (Quran 18:35). He had not wronged his friend or stolen anything. The injustice was to his own soul: "I do not think that this will ever perish. Nor do I believe that the Hour will ever come. And even if I am returned to my Lord, I will surely find better than this in return." (Quran 18:35-36) Three steps, in three sentences: the blessing becomes an identity, then a guarantee, then a theology.

3.The Friend Who Would Not Stay Silent

The believing companion could have stayed quiet — he had less, and the man with more was boasting to his face. Instead he answered, and went to the root: "Do you disbelieve in He who created you from dust and then from a sperm-drop and then proportioned you [as] a man? But as for me, He is Allah, my Lord, and I do not associate with my Lord anyone." (Quran 18:37-38)

Notice the method. First, the reminder of origin: you were dust, then a drop, then a man — you built nothing of yourself. Second, the plain declaration of his own position: as for me, Allah is my Lord, and I associate none with Him. He states his faith in one sentence, and the sentence draws the line: the gardens belong to Allah, and no blessing is shared with a partner.

This is the friendship the Quran honours. A friend is not only the one who celebrates your success; he is the one who tells you when your success is becoming your ruin. The companion had nothing to gain by speaking, and everything to lose. He spoke anyway, because the warning was the real gift.

4.The Sentence That Protects a Blessing

Then the companion gave him the sentence every Muslim now knows by heart — the heart of the story, and the reason this parable is remembered in every home:

وَلَوْلَآ إِذْ دَخَلْتَ جَنَّتَكَ قُلْتَ مَا شَآءَ ٱللَّهُ لَا قُوَّةَ إِلَّا بِٱللَّهِ ۚ إِن تَرَنِ أَنَا۠ أَقَلَّ مِنكَ مَالًۭا وَوَلَدًۭا

Quran 18:39

"And why, when you entered your garden, did you not say: 'What Allah has willed [has occurred]; there is no power except in Allah'? Even if you see me less than you in wealth and children..." This is where the words "Masha'Allah" come from when a Muslim admires something beautiful. The phrase is not a decoration. It returns the blessing to its Giver: not from my effort, not from my luck — from Allah, and only by His power does it stand.

The companion did not stop at the phrase. He named what could happen, in advance, so the warning could not be called unfair later: "It may be that my Lord will give me [something] better than your garden and will send upon it a calamity from the sky, and it will become a smooth, dusty ground. Or its water will become sunken [into the earth], so you would never be able to seek it." (Quran 18:40-41) Two ruins are named — a calamity from above, or water sinking below. The story is not about a man who was never warned. It is about a man who was warned clearly, kindly, and in time — and who did not listen.

5.And Then It Was Gone

The ruin came. The Quran does not say how it came, or how long it took — it simply says: "And his fruit was encompassed [by ruin], so he began to wring his hands over what he had spent on it while it had collapsed upon its trellises, and said: 'Oh, I wish I had not associated [anything] with my Lord!'" (Quran 18:42) The trellises fell with the vines.

Read his words carefully. He did not say, "I wish I had sold the gardens," or "I wish I had listened to my friend." He said, "I wish I had not associated anyone with my Lord." At the end, when the gardens were gone, he finally named the real sin. It was not the wealth. It was the shirk of the heart — the quiet belief that the blessing belonged to him, that it would never end, that the Hereafter would reward him anyway.

Then the verse that closes every excuse: "And he had no company to aid him other than Allah, nor could he defend himself." (Quran 18:43) The followers he had boasted about were nowhere. And then the last word: "There the authority is [completely] for Allah, the Truth. He is best in reward and best in outcome." (Quran 18:44)

6.The Valley of Gold

The Prophet ﷺ described the same disease in a hadith every reader of this story should know:

"If Adam's son had a valley full of gold, he would like to have two valleys, for nothing fills his mouth except dust. And Allah forgives him who repents to Him." — Sahih al-Bukhari 6439

A valley full of gold — not a coin, a whole valley between two mountains — and the son of Adam would want a second, and then a third. Nothing fills the hollow except the dust of the grave. The garden owner is that man. The wanting never stops on its own; it stops only when a person names it for what it is and turns back.

The Quran puts the same truth in the verse that follows the parable, and it is the verse that gives the whole passage its balance:

ٱلْمَالُ وَٱلْبَنُونَ زِينَةُ ٱلْحَيَوٰةِ ٱلدُّنْيَا ۖ وَٱلْبَٰقِيَٰتُ ٱلصَّٰلِحَٰتُ خَيْرٌ عِندَ رَبِّكَ ثَوَابًۭا وَخَيْرٌ أَمَلًۭا

Quran 18:46

"Wealth and children are the adornment of the life of this world. But the enduring good deeds are better in your Lord's sight for reward and better as [the foundation for] hopes." Wealth is not condemned here. It is called adornment — zinah, decoration, beauty. Adornment is not evil; it is not the foundation either. The enduring good deeds — the prayer, the charity, the truthful word, the kindness nobody saw — are the foundation, and they are the only part that remains when the adornment is taken away.

The same warning runs through the Quran in other words. "Competition in [worldly] increase diverts you until you visit the graves." (Quran 102:1-2) "Woe to every scorner and mocker who collects wealth and [continuously] counts it. He thinks that his wealth will make him immortal." (Quran 104:1-3) And the Prophet ﷺ gave the cure in one sentence: "Wealth is not in having many possessions, but true wealth is the richness of the soul." (Sahih al-Bukhari 6446; Sahih Muslim 1051) The parable asks which of the two men is actually rich.

7.Five Ways to Keep Wealth a Blessing

The parable is not a warning against owning things. The Prophet ﷺ and his Companions owned houses, land, and trade goods, and the Quran calls wealth an adornment, not a curse. The warning is against letting the adornment become the foundation. Here are five practices from the story:

1. Say Masha'Allah when you admire anything. Your home, your car, your child, your business — say it out loud, and mean it: what Allah has willed has happened, and there is no power except in Allah (Quran 18:39). The sentence is small, and it is the whole defence.

2. Give from it before you are asked. The garden owner's wealth stayed in his hands and became his god. Wealth that flows out in charity — zakat first, then sadaqah — cannot harden into an idol. Give a fixed share of every income, promptly and without waiting to be asked.

3. Never measure a person by it. The owner's first sin was a comparison: "I am greater than you in wealth." The moment wealth becomes the measure of a human being, every poor person becomes lesser and every rich person becomes greater — and the believer who thinks that way has left the Quran's measure behind: taqwa, not net worth.

4. Plan, but trust Allah. The gardens were planted, watered, and worked — the owner did his part, and Islam asks no less. But the work is the means, not the guarantee. A narration reported in Jami' at-Tirmidhi carries the Prophet's ﷺ instruction: tie your camel, then trust in Allah. Do the work with both hands, and leave the outcome with the One who sends the rain.

5. Invest in what remains. "The enduring good deeds are better in your Lord's sight for reward and better as [the foundation for] hopes." (Quran 18:46) Every rupee spent on a good deed is a rupee no calamity can destroy. The gardens fell on their trellises. The good deeds do not fall. Build the part of your life that no ruin can reach, and let the rest be adornment — enjoyed, thanked for, and held loosely.

Two gardens, one boast, and a ruin that came after a clear warning. The parable ends with a sentence that is also a beginning: authority belongs to Allah, the Truth, and He is best in reward and best in outcome (Quran 18:44). May Allah protect our blessings with gratitude, keep our wealth from becoming our measure, and make our enduring good deeds heavier than our adornments. And may we learn the lesson while the gardens are still standing.
