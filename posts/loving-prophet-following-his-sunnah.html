Loving the Prophet ﷺ: Love Shown by Following His Sunnah

R Razia Azeem · 2 Nov, 2026

Every Muslim says it, and says it sincerely: I love the Prophet. But love, in Islam, is not only a feeling in the heart. It is a direction in life. The Quran gives it a test, the companions lived it out loud, and the test is simpler — and harder — than we like to admit: if you love him, follow him.

Daily Life

Ask a Muslim child who he loves most after Allah, and the answer comes without hesitation. Ask an adult the same question, and the answer is the same — but the adult knows something the child does not yet: love is easy to declare and hard to prove. We prove love for a parent by obedience when it costs us something. We prove love for a friend by showing up when it is inconvenient. And love for the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) has its own proof, stated plainly in the Quran and lived dramatically by his companions: following him.

This article is not about celebrating love in words. It is about what that love asks of an ordinary Tuesday — at work, at home, in anger, in trade, in speech — and about the promise attached to it.

1.Love as a Condition of Faith

The claim is strong, and it is worth hearing in full before softening it. A narration reported in Sahih al-Bukhari, on the authority of Anas ibn Malik (may Allah be pleased with him), carries the Prophet's words in meaning: none of you truly believes until I am more beloved to him than his father, his children, and all of mankind. Scholars explain this carefully: it is not about the natural, instinctive affection a parent feels for a child, which no one chooses. It is about the voluntary, chosen love that governs decisions — the love that decides, when your own desire and his guidance point in different directions, which one wins.

That is why the narration speaks of faith itself, not of extra credit. If his guidance is only followed when it agrees with what you already wanted, then it is not guidance at all — it is decoration. Faith, in this narration, means his word outranks your preference. And the reward matches the demand: a narration in Sahih Muslim (2832) carries the meaning that among those who love him most will be people who come after him, one of whom would wish to have seen him even at the expense of his family and his wealth. We are those people — we never saw him, and yet the longing is asked of us as proof of faith.

Say, if you love Allah, then follow me, so Allah will love you and forgive you your sins. — Quran 3:31, in meaning

2.The Quran's Test: Follow Me

Quran 3:31 is the verse that turns love from a slogan into a test. In meaning, Allah says: say, if you love Allah, then follow me — and the result follows in the same breath: Allah will love you and forgive you your sins. Notice the order. It does not say "feel love, and you are loved." It says "follow, and you are loved." The path to Allah's love runs through the Prophet's example, and the forgiveness is attached to the following, not to the feeling.

A second verse gives the example its scope. In the meaning of Quran 33:21, there is in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern of conduct for anyone whose hope is in Allah and the Last Day and who remembers Allah much. A pattern is not a single act; it is a whole way of being, repeated until it becomes character. And a third verse draws the line without apology. In the meaning of Quran 9:24, if your fathers, your sons, your brothers, your wives, your kindred, the wealth you have acquired, the commerce you fear may decline, and the dwellings you delight in are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and striving in His cause, then wait until Allah brings His command. Love, here, is measured at the exact point where it costs you something you also love.

3.Umar and the Last Fortress: Yourself

The companions understood this test in their own lives, and one conversation shows it better than any lecture. Umar ibn al-Khattab (may Allah be pleased with him) once told the Prophet, in a narration reported in Sahih al-Bukhari, that he was more beloved to him than everything — except his own self. The Prophet answered, in meaning: not yet, Umar, until I am more beloved to you than your own self. Umar paused, reconsidered, and said that now he was more beloved to him than his own self. The Prophet said: now, Umar.

Why is the self the last fortress? Because a father, a child, a fortune — you can love the Prophet more than all of them in principle and still keep one exception: yourself. Your comfort, your reputation, your habit, your pride. Loving him more than yourself means that when his guidance asks you to apologise first, to lose a deal rather than lie, to forgive when revenge would feel better, you do it — not because it is easy, but because he is more beloved than the self that resists it. Umar's story is not a standard for a single dramatic moment. It is a daily question: today, in this one decision, who is more beloved — him, or me?

4.What Following Looks Like on an Ordinary Day

Following the sunnah is often imagined as a list of rituals, and rituals matter — but the Quran describes the Prophet's character before his rituals. In the meaning of Quran 68:4, he is upon an exalted standard of character. His sunnah, then, is first of all a character: truthful in speech, faithful to promises, gentle with the weak, patient under insult, generous without display. A narration reported in Sunan al-Tirmidhi carries the meaning that a smile to your brother is charity. That is sunnah too — not a grand gesture, but a face that makes a home easier to live in.

On an ordinary day, following looks like this: greeting with salam before being greeted; speaking truthfully when a small lie would be smoother; keeping a promise to a child, not only to a client; eating with gratitude and moderation; guarding the tongue about an absent person; praying on time even when work is pressing; and sending blessings upon him, as Quran 33:56 invites believers to do — the subject of our article on the blessings of darood. None of these acts is dramatic. Together, they are the pattern of Quran 33:21, stitched into a life. The sunnah is not a costume worn on Fridays; it is the way a person handles Tuesday.

There is also a sunnah of learning. You cannot follow a pattern you have never studied. Reading the Seerah — even a few pages a week — turns a name you revere into a life you recognise: how he treated servants, how he forgave enemies, how he played with children, how he bore grief. Love grows with knowledge, and knowledge without practice stays admiration. The two must travel together.

5.You Will Be With Those You Love

There is a narration that the companions cherished above almost any other, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim on the authority of Anas. A man asked the Prophet about the Hour, and the Prophet asked him, in meaning, what he had prepared for it. The man answered that he had not prepared much prayer or fasting or charity — but he loved Allah and His Messenger. The Prophet said, in meaning: you will be with those whom you love. Anas later said that the companions never rejoiced over anything as they rejoiced over those words.

Why did they rejoice? Because the promise turns love into destiny. The company you keep in your heart decides the company you keep in the next life. But the same narration carries its own warning, and it is the warning of this whole article: love that never imitates is hard to call love. The man in the narration did not claim perfection; he claimed love, and love, honestly held, pulls a person towards the beloved's ways. If your love for the Prophet never changes a single habit, it is worth asking — gently, not harshly — whether it is love or only admiration. Real love imitates. That is why following is not the opposite of loving; it is loving, grown up.

6.Seven Days of Turning Love Into Following

Here is a small, concrete way to begin this week — one step a day, each one a piece of the pattern:

Day 1 — Pick one small sunnah and keep it all week. A greeting offered first, a meal begun with gratitude, a phone call to a relative. Small and kept beats grand and abandoned.

Day 2 — Send blessings upon him deliberately. Set aside a few minutes for salawat, not rushed between tasks, remembering that Quran 33:56 joins Allah's own blessings to the believers' — and read our darood article for its virtues.

Day 3 — Read one incident from the Seerah. Choose how he forgave, how he kept a promise, or how he treated someone weaker than himself, and ask: what would this change in my day?

Day 4 — Win one clash. Notice a moment when your desire and his guidance disagree — an angry reply, a dishonest shortcut, a prayer delayed — and choose his guidance once, consciously.

Day 5 — Let character be sunnah. Practise the sunnah of gentleness at home, where no audience watches; for more on this, see our article on good character as the heaviest deed.

Day 6 — Teach one sunnah to a child. A short dua, a manner of eating, a habit of greeting. What you teach, you remember.

Day 7 — Make the dua of love. Ask Allah, in your own words, to make the Prophet more beloved to you than yourself — Umar's prayer, in your own life — and to make following him easy for you.

Love for the Prophet is not proved by how loudly we declare it, but by how faithfully we follow it. Follow him, and the Quran's promise follows you: Allah will love you, and forgive you your sins. For daily Islamic articles grounded in the Quran and Hadith, visit our blog.
